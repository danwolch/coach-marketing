# Work Coach — Raw Emotions

Working notes for the marketing campaign. Emotions only — no formats, no calendar yet.

Sources: the 60 playbooks, the landing pages, the homepage app demo, and the Mac app's onboarding, paywall, and coaching-insight copy.

---

## The through-line

**Confusion appears at every stage, in a different costume each time.**

*Why is no one replying / what did I say wrong / how does this place actually work / is this PIP real / what does "ready" even mean / am I any good at managing.*

Same root cause every time: **nobody will tell you the truth, and the person who knows won't say it.**

Confusion is the hook — an unanswered question in someone's head demands an answer. Relief is the close — it's the only thing on this list the product can hand someone in five minutes.

---

## The basic emotions

Named plainly, with the sentence actually in the person's head.

| Emotion | The sentence |
|---|---|
| **Confusion** | "What did I do wrong? Why didn't they make an offer? What did I say that was wrong?" |
| **Frustration** | "I did everything right and still didn't get it." |
| **Self-doubt** | "Am I not good enough?" |
| **Embarrassment** | "I sounded stupid." |
| **Anger** | "They wasted my time." |
| **Fear** | "What if none of them come through?" |
| **Envy** | "Why did they get it and I didn't?" |
| **Loneliness** | "There's no one who can tell me." |
| **Hope** | "This one felt good." |
| **Relief** | "Finally, someone told me." |
| **Pride** | "I nailed it." |

Notes:
- **Confusion** is the biggest. It's an unanswered question, and it's the one thing Work Coach can actually answer.
- **Self-doubt** is the most powerful and the easiest to get wrong. Push on it and people feel worse and blame you for it.
- **Hope** is the only up emotion in a job search, which is exactly why the no hurts as much as it does.
- **Relief** and **pride** are the payoff emotions. What buying feels like, and what it's for.

## By lifecycle stage

### Applying

- Confusion — "Why is no one responding?"
- Frustration — "I meet every requirement and got auto-rejected in four minutes."
- Fear — "Am I even employable?" / "How long do the savings last?"
- Envy — "Another 'excited to announce' post."
- Loneliness — "No one knows how my day went, including me."

**Dominant: confusion, in the form where you don't even know a human saw it.** Silence at scale. Almost no hope at this stage, which makes it the most demoralizing one.

### Interviewing

- Confusion — "What did I say wrong? Why didn't they make an offer?"
- Embarrassment — "I rambled." / "I blanked on a question I know the answer to."
- Hope — "This one felt good."
- Fear — "They're going to ask about the gap."
- Anger — "Four rounds and a take-home, then nothing."
- Self-doubt — arrives around the third or fourth no

**Dominant: confusion and embarrassment.** Highest hope of any stage, which is why the no lands hardest here.

### Onboarding

- Fear — "They're going to figure out I can't do this."
- Confusion — "How does anything actually work here? Who decides?"
- Embarrassment — "I've asked this same question three times."
- Fear — "Did I make a mistake leaving my last job?"
- Loneliness — "I don't have anyone here yet."

**Dominant: fear of being found out.** The trap: the only way to learn how things work is to ask, and asking is the thing you're afraid of.

### PIP

- Fear — "Am I about to lose my job?" Mortgage, visa, insurance.
- Confusion — "Is this real, or have they already decided?"
- Anger — "Why is this the first I'm hearing of it?"
- Shame — "I can't tell anyone. Not my friends, not my partner."
- Loneliness — HR isn't yours, your manager isn't yours, your team can't know
- Self-doubt — "What if they're right?"

**Dominant: fear, shame, and total isolation.** Highest intensity anywhere on this list, and the most delicate to touch. The shame means the wrong tone gets you hated.

### Promotion application

- Confusion — "What does 'ready' actually mean here?"
- Frustration — "I've done the work and nobody noticed."
- Envy — "They got it and they're not better than me."
- Self-doubt — "Am I overestimating myself?"
- Fear — "If I ask and they say no, I look arrogant."
- Anger — after a denial delivered as one vague sentence

**Dominant: envy and frustration.** What makes this stage different: you're competing against people you like and sit next to.

### Managing

- Loneliness — "I can't say this to my reports, my boss, or my peers."
- Dread — "I have to have that conversation this week."
- Guilt — "I'm failing this person."
- Confusion — "Am I even good at this? Nobody tells me."
- Fear — "I'm going to lose them."
- Imposter — "I was promoted for work I no longer do."

**Dominant: loneliness and dread.** Guilt appears only here — the one stage where the pain is about someone else rather than yourself.

### Cross-stage observations

- **Intensity peaks at PIP. Hope peaks at interviewing. Envy only really bites at promotion. Guilt exists only in managing.**
- **Three stages get searched at 1am:** interviewing, PIP, promotion denial. Onboarding and managing are felt but not searched — people don't have the words yet. Applying isn't searched either; it's scrolled.

## Layers

These aren't stages. They sit on top of all six and change the temperature of each.

### Non-native English speaker

- Frustration — "I know the answer. I just can't get it out fast enough."
- Fear — "They think I'm not smart."
- Anger — "Someone said my idea back, worse, and got credit for it."
- Embarrassment — "They said 'sorry, what?' and I had to say it again."
- Self-silencing — "I had something to say and the moment passed." Forty times a day.
- Exhaustion — "By 4pm I have nothing left." Working all day in a second language is genuinely tiring, and nobody names it.
- Confusion — "Everyone nodded, so I nodded." The idiom you looked up afterward.
- Loneliness — "I'm in the room but not in the conversation."
- Ceiling fear — "I'll never get past this level, because the next one is all presenting."
- Relief — "They understood me the first time."

**Dominant: frustration at the gap between what you know and what comes out.** Underneath it: the fear of being judged on the wrong thing entirely.

What's structurally different about this group: **they're not confused about what happened.** They know exactly what happened, watched it happen in real time, and couldn't stop it. Every other stage's core wound is not knowing.

By stage:
- **Applying** — a name and a visa line on the résumé, and no way to know if that's why it's silent.
- **Interviewing** — the highest-stakes language performance of your life, judged by someone who's never had to do it in theirs.
- **Onboarding** — new people, new jargon, new company idioms, all at the moment you're most afraid to ask.
- **PIP** — *"If I lose this job I lose the country."* The visa. Highest-stakes fear anywhere in these notes, by a distance.
- **Promotion** — the ceiling made concrete. "Strong engineer, not ready for the leadership piece" often means "doesn't present well in English," and nobody will say it out loud.
- **Managing** — hard feedback in a second language, where it all rides on nuance and one blunt word lands wrong.

⚠️ Tension to handle carefully: `english-at-work.html` says, correctly, *"your accent was never the problem, and we're not here to change it."* But plenty of people here do feel shame about their accent. The emotion is layered — ashamed of something they've been told not to be ashamed of. Get it wrong and it reads as dismissive or as an accent-reduction pitch. Both are bad.

### First-generation professional

Nobody in your family has done this job.

- Confusion — "Everyone else seems to know rules that nobody told me."
- Shame — "I can't ask, because asking shows I don't know."
- Loneliness — "My family is proud of me and there's nothing they can do." They think you've made it. You can't tell them you're lost.
- Frustration — "The people getting ahead had someone explaining it to them the whole time."
- Fear — "I can't afford to get this wrong." No safety net, nobody's basement to move back into. So you play it safe in a career that pays out for risk.
- Over-gratitude — "I'm just happy to be here." Why you didn't negotiate, didn't ask, didn't push. Costs real money.
- Envy — "He knew what a skip-level was on day one."
- Imposter — the standard version, but with an actual basis: you genuinely were missing information other people were handed.

**Dominant: not knowing there was a game.** Not losing it — not knowing it was being played.

The angle is already ours. `about.html`: *"Work Coach exists because not everyone has a Jane."* This audience never had one. And the PwC story is this story exactly — *"I had been putting my head down, doing good work, assuming it would speak for itself. It didn't."* First-gen mistake in one sentence, told by the founder. No competitor can copy that.

By stage:
- **Applying** — you don't know referrals are the actual door, so you apply cold and conclude you're not good enough.
- **Interviewing** — you don't know it's a performance. You answer honestly instead of well. You don't know you're allowed to ask them questions.
- **Onboarding** — you don't know the unwritten rules, or that you're allowed to ask for anything.
- **PIP** — you don't know severance is negotiable, that an hour with an employment lawyer is normal, or that you can ask what "meeting the bar" means in writing.
- **Promotion** — you don't know promotions are *asked for*. Sharpest one, and exactly what `get-promoted.html` attacks: "work hard and it'll happen" is how people stay stuck.
- **Managing** — you've never had a good manager to copy, so you're improvising the entire job.

### Other layers not yet worked through

- **Older workers (45+)** — most commercially underserved group in the category. Interviewed by someone half your age, "culture fit," "overqualified." Nobody markets to them and they can pay.
- **Introverts and slow processors** — biggest self-identifying audience, zero political risk, already written in `speak-up-in-meetings` (Deb Liu: "by the time I process, the conversation has moved on").
- **Women** — strongest documented case, since the vague-feedback problem the product attacks is measurably worse here. Also the most crowded and easiest to get wrong.
- **Recently laid off** — different from job searching. Involuntary, a severance clock, and a shame that voluntary job seekers don't carry.

## Appendix: sharper first-person variants

Same emotions, written as the sentence at 1am rather than the category. Raw material — a register to draw on, not a replacement for the plain list above.

- *"What did I say wrong?"* — not the whole interview. One sentence. You know exactly which one and you've replayed it forty times.
- *"I looked at their office on Google Maps."* Hope you're embarrassed to have had. You imagined telling people. You knew better than to hope and hoped anyway, so the rejection lands twice — once for the job, once for being stupid enough to want it.
- *"I'm going to have to be a version of myself I don't like."* The performance/authenticity split. You can feel yourself being fake and you suspect they can too.
- *"If they just worked with me for a week they'd hire me."* The frustration of being reduced to 45 minutes.
- *"I am a 42-year-old professional being graded by a 29-year-old."* The status inversion. Gets worse the more senior you are.
- *"Was it something about me, or was the role already filled?"* Suspicion, not curiosity.
- *"I'm lying to everyone every day."* Searching while employed. Camera off, dentist appointment, a calendar block that says "focus time."
- *"What if they're right?"* Month four. The erosion. Where people either buy something or give up.

## Open questions

- Which stages carry the campaign? Interviewing and PIP have the most voltage, but PIP is a knife.
- Are the layers their own audiences with their own campaigns, or modifiers applied inside the interviewing and promotion stages?
- Which audience: the person interviewing well and losing anyway, or the person who knows they're bad at it? Different people, different emotions.
