How to explain a career gap in an interview

The answer is shorter than you think, and the interviewer cares about less of it than you fear. Here's how to say it once and move on.

12 min read

Two things make a career gap worse than it is: rehearsing a long defense nobody asked for, and assuming the interviewer has already decided something about you. Most of them haven't, and what they're waiting to see is whether you can say what happened without getting weird about it.

This article covers the kinds of gap and why interviewers react to them differently, and the four-sentence answer that handles almost all of them. It also covers how the gap shows up on your resume and your LinkedIn, what to do if you were fired, and how to practice until you sound unbothered. Write the answer first, then say it out loud twenty times, because the delivery is what people actually react to.

The advice comes from Nikhyl Singhal, who ran product at Meta and Credit Karma and now hosts The Skip; Lauren Ipsen, who recruits senior executives at Daversa Partners; Phyl Terry, whose book Never Search Alone has organized thousands of job seekers into councils; Deb Liu, CEO of Ancestry; Aaron Francis and John Bonini, both laid off and both willing to describe it in detail; and Jonathan Black, who directs the careers service at Oxford University. Every video quote plays the exact moment it was said.

Not every gap gets the same reaction

Most people talk about "career gap" as if it were one thing, but interviewers sort it into categories. Some gaps barely register and some need a sentence of context, and knowing which one you have tells you how much to prepare.

A layoff is the easiest, and it's now the most common. Since 2022 whole teams have gone at once in tech, media, and finance, and every hiring manager you'll meet either did layoffs or survived them. Caregiving and parental leave are close behind, because everyone understands what a sick parent or a new baby does to a calendar. A planned sabbatical or a period of study is fine as long as you can say what it was for. A failed startup is usually a plus with founders and a question mark with big companies, so the framing changes depending on who's across from you.

The harder ones are health and burnout, where you're weighing privacy against explanation, and being fired for cause, which is the only category where the reason genuinely matters to the person hiring you.

One more thing worth knowing before you write anything: the shame you feel about the gap is usually out of proportion to how the other side sees it. Jonathan Black has spent years advising people through exactly this moment and says it plainly.

"So again, I think the advice there is not to be ashamed. This is quite a common thing, and most people you meet will have been through this once or more in their lifetime."
Jonathan Black, director of the Oxford University Careers Service · Working It, Financial Times (12:46, audio)

What the interviewer is actually checking

You're afraid they think you were the weak one on the list, and that the gap proves something is wrong with you. What they're usually checking is much narrower, and it's mostly about risk to them.

They want to know that whatever caused the gap is over, that you're available and not still half-committed elsewhere, that you haven't gone stale on the work, and that you can talk about a hard thing like an adult. That last one carries more weight than people realize. An interviewer who watches you get defensive about a layoff will wonder what you're like when a project goes sideways, and that's a fair thing for them to wonder.

So the gap question is closer to a composure test than a background check. Answer in four sentences and move on and you've cleared it. What causes trouble is talking for three minutes, or hinting at a grievance you won't name.

What they're checking

  • Is the thing that caused the gap finished?
  • Are you available now, and for how long?
  • Have your skills kept up?
  • Can you discuss a setback without heat?

What they're mostly not checking

  • Whether you were on the list because you were the worst performer.
  • The private medical or family detail behind a break.
  • Whether you should have found something faster.

How to build the answer

Almost every version of this question is handled by the same three beats, in this order: what happened, what you did with the time, why you're ready now. Then you stop and let them ask.

What happened is one sentence, and it names a cause outside your performance where that's true. "My role was cut when the company reduced headcount by about a third." "I took eight months out to care for a parent who was ill." You don't need a preamble and you don't need an apology.

Aaron Francis was laid off from PlanetScale while on paternity leave with his second set of twins. Notice how he describes it: as a business decision with a logic you can follow, with nobody cast as the villain.

The one-sentence business reason. The player below is queued to 7:10, the exact moment Aaron Francis explains why his whole team went. Watch on YouTube.

"The company decides, whoops, we don't want free users anymore, actually our free tier is going away, there's no free tier ever at all whatsoever. And the guy that makes YouTube videos that brings in hundreds of free tier users, we maybe don't need him anymore either. And so me and Steve and the marketing team and the sales team, we all got laid off. Everybody got laid off, only engineers were left."
Aaron Francis, co-founder of Try Hard Studios · Startup Dad

That's the tone to copy. The company changed strategy and the roles attached to the old strategy went with it, and there's nothing in that to defend. If your layoff had a reason like that, say the reason.

What you did with the time is one or two sentences of evidence: contract work, a certification, a side project, an open source contribution, running a house through a family crisis. If the honest answer is that you spent the first stretch recovering and the rest applying, say a short version of that and move on. Making something up is worse than a plain gap, because thin stories fall apart under follow-up questions.

Why you're ready now is the sentence that closes it. "That's resolved, I've been full-time on the search since March, and this role is exactly the kind of platform work I want to get back to." It tells them the risk is gone and hands the conversation back.

The answer, four sentences

  • "My role was cut in October when the company laid off about 30% of the org, including most of my team."
  • "Since then I've done two contract projects for a health tech startup and finished a data engineering course I'd been putting off."
  • "I've been looking full-time since January and I'm ready to start straight away."
  • "Happy to go into any part of that in more detail."

Then be quiet. The silence after a short answer feels much longer to you than to them, and filling it is where people talk themselves into trouble. If you want more on the opening story this connects to, the tell me about yourself playbook covers the two-minute version of your whole arc.

What you did with the time matters more than how long it was

The middle beat is the one people skimp on, and it's the one doing the most work. Nikhyl Singhal spent an episode of The Skip on how hiring managers read early-career resumes, and his comparison applies at any level. Two candidates with similar gaps get read completely differently based on what they can describe.

Two ways of describing the same gap. The player below is queued to 31:30, the exact moment Nikhyl Singhal contrasts the two candidates. Watch on YouTube.

"If someone comes to me and says, well, I worked at this large company, and then they had a way of doing something, and so I sort of worked on it, and then a couple of years later, I got laid off. And then you compare that to someone who's like, well, you know, we didn't have any tools, so I ended up having to build things myself or use things that were off the shelf, and here's all this latest, and then I improved it over time because the technology and the tools are getting better. You're going to be like, well, that person is a lot more modern."
Nikhyl Singhal, host of The Skip, former Meta VP of Product · The Skip

Both of those people have a gap, and only one of them sounds current. If you're in a gap right now and the interviews haven't started, that's the thing to fix first: get one piece of recent, describable work, even small and unpaid, so the middle sentence has something in it. Six weeks of that changes the answer permanently.

How the gap looks on your resume and LinkedIn

The written version has a different job from the spoken one. On paper you're just trying to keep the reader from stopping, so make the gap easy to take in at a glance instead of explaining it.

Use years instead of months on your resume if that closes a gap of a few months honestly. If it's longer, put a line in your work history for what you were doing, with dates, the same way you'd list a job: "Career break, caregiving, 2024 to 2025" or "Independent consulting, 2024 to present," with a bullet or two of real work underneath. Recruiters skimming for continuity will read that and keep going. An unexplained blank is what makes them slow down and guess.

On LinkedIn, the Career Break entry exists for exactly this and using it is fine. Add a reason if you have one you're comfortable naming, and set your headline to what you do rather than to your last employer, so people see the work and not the exit. If you were part of a public layoff, a short post about it is worth doing, because recruiters search those threads.

Two things to avoid. Don't stretch dates, because background checks compare against payroll records and a fudge that gets caught costs you the offer. And don't leave your last role open-ended if it has ended, because that looks like an oversight and earns you a suspicious first question. If you're also changing fields, the resume for a career change playbook covers how to make the whole document tell one story.

If you were fired rather than laid off

This is the case people most want a script for, and it's the one where honesty pays best, because a story that contradicts a reference check ends the process.

The workable version has three parts and is shorter than you want it to be. Name what happened in neutral language: "I was let go." Give the real reason in one line without arguing your side of it: "My manager and I disagreed about direction, and after two quarters they decided it wasn't working." Then say what changed since, in the past tense, as something you've already dealt with.

What sinks people is arguing the case. If you spend ninety seconds explaining that your manager was unreasonable, the interviewer stops thinking about your old job and starts imagining how you'd describe them to your next employer. Lauren Ipsen, who places executives for a living, is blunt about this: never blame your counterparts in an interview, whatever the history. You can be honest about a mismatch without assigning fault.

The fit framing is often the truest one available. Wes Bush was fired from Vidyard after repeatedly pushing ideas his leadership didn't want to pursue, and went on to build ProductLed.

Delivering Value: I was fired, then started my business, with Wes Bush. This one is audio only. The quote is at 23:37; the player starts from the top, or open it on Apple Podcasts cued to 23:37.

"Back to Vidyard, it's like, I'm so glad I got fired because it wasn't the right environment for me to succeed in. I thrive in a much different environment. And now I can kind of craft that environment."
Wes Bush, CEO of ProductLed · Delivering Value (23:37, audio)

Said in an interview that becomes: "It wasn't the right environment for the way I work, and I understood that better afterwards than I did at the time." It's honest and short, and it doesn't ask the interviewer to take sides.

One practical note. Check what your former employer's HR will confirm, which at most large companies is dates and title only. Knowing that stops you over-explaining out of fear of what they might say.

When the gap has gone on a long time

A year or more out is a different conversation, mostly because of what it's done to you rather than what it looks like to them. Phyl Terry has coached thousands of job seekers, including senior executives who spent a year getting nowhere, and he stops mid-episode to say this directly to anyone in that position.

What a long gap does to your head. The player below is queued to 1:10:18, the exact moment Phyl Terry says it. Watch on YouTube.

"Then you're going to get paralyzed and feel like you're really worthless. And if anyone watching this has been out of work for a while and feels that, let me just tell you: you are not worthless. You are not worthless. And I want you to invest in yourself to prove that to yourself, that you're not worthless, you are worth the investment of this time and energy."
Phyl Terry, author of Never Search Alone · Lenny's Podcast

Practically, a long gap needs the middle beat to carry more weight, plus a reason the search ran this long that isn't about you. Terry's term for the second one is candidate market fit: what the market will pay for right now, which in a down market can sit a level below what you did before. Saying "I spent the first six months looking for the same title I had, and it took me a while to accept the market had moved" is an answer hiring managers recognize as true.

Interview sequencing helps too. Singhal advises running your search in waves, starting several processes in the same two or three weeks rather than one at a time.

Running interviews in waves. The player below is queued to 39:24, the exact moment Nikhyl Singhal describes the pipeline. Watch on YouTube.

"I'm going to time them, so we start all of them around roughly the same time period. Two, three weeks in, we're all in motion together. I'm interviewing, I'm active, I'm practicing my story, and so I'm getting better because it's intense. It doesn't come across that I have nothing else going on. I come across as in demand."
Nikhyl Singhal, host of The Skip, former Meta VP of Product · The Skip

That does two things for a long gap. Your answer gets fluent fast, because you're giving it four times a month instead of once a quarter, and you genuinely do have other things going on, which changes how you sound without you having to act.

Practicing until it doesn't sound defensive

You can have the right words and still fail this question, because what people react to is your tone. John Bonini was laid off from Litmus in 2017, weeks after his honeymoon and with his wife pregnant, and he's honest that it took him a while before he could walk into an interview as himself.

Showing up as yourself again. The player below is queued to 24:18, the exact moment John Bonini describes it. Watch on YouTube.

"Yeah, absolutely. I took it personally. It took me a little bit to like regroup and to be able to show up in another interview as me and not as the guy with his tail between his legs, so to speak."
John Bonini, founder of Content Brands · Delivering Value

That regrouping is a practice problem more than a feelings problem. The gap answer is the one thing you've never said out loud in a friendly setting, so the first time you say it is under pressure, to a stranger who's evaluating you. Lauren Ipsen watches strong candidates burn real opportunities relearning how to talk about themselves.

The tenth-interview effect. The player below is queued to 54:19, the exact moment Lauren Ipsen describes it. Watch on YouTube.

"Being able to tell your story, like practice telling your story. So often people don't do that, especially when they're not looking for jobs. And then it's not until their tenth interview that they know how to tell their story and what they did and what they impacted and why they were someone to be missed when they moved on."
Lauren Ipsen, executive recruiter at Daversa Partners · Lenny's Podcast

So build those reps before the interviews that count. Record the answer on your phone and play it back, which is uncomfortable and also the fastest way to hear defensiveness you can't feel while you're talking. Listen for the tells: your voice going up at the end, a laugh you didn't mean, the word "unfortunately," any sentence starting with "to be fair." Time it, and cut anything past forty seconds. Then get a friend to ask you cold, in the middle of another conversation, because that's how it actually arrives.

Deb Liu ran product at Facebook and PayPal before becoming CEO of Ancestry, and she's watched closely which people come out of setbacks in better shape.

Stumbling blocks into stepping stones. The player below is queued to 11:47, the exact moment Deb Liu says it. Watch on YouTube.

"The people who are most successful are not the people that had no failures, who lived charmed lives and had up-and-to-the-right careers and got promoted every cycle. The people who were most successful were the ones who actually, through adversity, learned to turn stumbling blocks into stepping stones. They were the ones who got hard feedback and then came back stronger because now they learned what to do differently."
Deb Liu, CEO of Ancestry and former VP of Product at Facebook · Lenny's Podcast

You don't have to make that case in the interview, and you shouldn't try. Just say the four sentences calmly, and the calm makes the point for you. If the rejections are wearing you down while you practice, the staying confident through rejection playbook has the routines that help.

Frequently asked questions

How do I explain being laid off in an interview?

Name it as a business decision in one sentence, with a number if you have one: "My role was cut when the company reduced headcount by about a third in March." Add what you've done since, then say you're available now. Don't apologize, and don't hint that it was political. The volume of layoffs since 2022 means every interviewer has seen this, and a matter-of-fact answer is the whole test.

Do I have to say why I took a career break?

You need to say something, but you choose the level of detail. "I took time out for a family health matter, which is now resolved" is a complete answer and no reasonable interviewer will push past it. If they do keep digging into private medical or family detail, you can say "I'd rather keep the specifics private, but it's fully resolved and I'm available full-time," and then answer their next question. How they respond to that boundary tells you something useful about them.

Should I put a career gap on my resume?

Yes, if it's longer than a few months. Add a line with dates the way you'd list a role, labeled for what it was, with a bullet or two of anything real you did. Recruiters get through a labeled break faster than a blank one, and a blank raises a question the document can't answer. Never adjust dates to hide it, since background checks compare against payroll records.

How honest should I be about being fired?

Be honest about the fact and brief about the reason, then finish with what changed. Say you were let go, give the real cause in a line, and describe the fix in the past tense. Skip your side of the argument entirely. References and background checks often surface the truth anyway, and what actually costs people the offer is the distance between their story and the employer's.

What if my gap is two years or more?

Lead with the recent evidence rather than the timeline. Any contract work, volunteering, study, or project from the last few months does more for you than an explanation of the earlier months. Then give a straightforward reason the search ran long, which is often that you were targeting the level you left at while the market had moved. Aim your applications where you're currently hireable and move back up from inside a job.

Will a career gap stop me getting hired?

On its own, rarely. What costs people offers is the delivery: an answer that runs three minutes, a story that shifts between rounds, or visible resentment about the old employer. A short consistent answer plus something recent you can point to removes the gap as a topic within the first ten minutes.

The gap answer on one page

Before you apply

  • Write the four sentences: what happened, what you did with the time, why you're ready now, an offer to say more.
  • Get one piece of recent describable work, even small and unpaid, so the middle sentence has content.
  • Label the break on your resume with dates, and add a Career Break entry on LinkedIn.
  • Check what your former employer's HR will confirm.

Before the interview

  • Record the answer on your phone and play it back, listening for the tells.
  • Time it, and cut anything past forty seconds.
  • Have a friend ask you cold, out of nowhere, so you've felt the surprise once.
  • Start several processes in the same two or three weeks so you're getting reps.

In the interview

  • Answer in four sentences, then stop and let them ask.
  • Don't blame anyone, including yourself.
  • If they press on private detail, restate the boundary once and move on.
  • Bring the conversation back to recent work as soon as it's natural.

Afterwards

  • If two interviewers dug into the same sentence, rewrite that sentence.
  • Keep the answer identical across rounds, because they compare notes.
