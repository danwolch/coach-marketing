How to prepare for a product sense (or product thinking) interview

One ambiguous prompt, about 35 minutes, and an interviewer scoring five specific things. This is the structure that covers all five, and how to practice it.

13 min read

Are you reading sample answers and hoping the prompt turns out to be one you've seen? Then the interviewer says "design a product for gardeners," and because silence feels dangerous, you start listing ideas within thirty seconds. That's the most common way to fail this interview, and it has nothing to do with how good your ideas are.

This article covers what interviewers actually score, and the three shapes the questions come in. It also covers a step-by-step structure for answering, a worked example you can copy the mechanics from, and a practice plan. Read the structure section twice, then spend your remaining prep time saying answers out loud, because reading quietly doesn't prepare you to talk for 35 minutes.

The advice comes from Ben Erez, who interviewed more than 50 PM candidates at Meta and now coaches product interviews full time; Julie Zhuo, who spent 14 years at Facebook and became its VP of product design; Shreyas Doshi, who led product teams at Stripe, Twitter, and Google; Scott Belsky, Adobe's chief product officer at the time; Jiaona Zhang, then head of product at Webflow; and hiring leaders from Netflix, Rippling, and EvenUp. Every quote plays the exact moment it was said.

What interviewers actually score

Product sense interviews, sometimes called product thinking or product design interviews, were popularized by Meta and Google and have spread across the industry, including Stripe, OpenAI, Block, and plenty of earlier-stage companies. The setup is consistent: a 45-minute slot, roughly 35 usable minutes after introductions, and one open-ended prompt.

Ben Erez, who sat on the interviewer side of this exact loop at Meta, describes a rubric with five dimensions. You need a passing score on each one, and a brilliant performance on one can't rescue a miss on another:

  • Clear communication: an easy-to-follow answer with visible structure.
  • Product motivation: you examine why the product exists and what it's trying to do before building anything.
  • Segmentation: you define the audience, split it into meaningfully different groups, and pick one on purpose.
  • Problem identification: you list real pain points for that group and prioritize one.
  • Solution development: you brainstorm more than one approach, pick with reasoning, and scope a concrete V1.

One more thing worth knowing about the person across the table: they're usually a working PM squeezing your interview between meetings, filling out that rubric from memory afterward. Your job is to make their evidence easy to collect. Announce what you're about to do, do it, then check in before moving on. Erez calls this waypointing, and it's the easiest part of the rubric to score well on.

Note that this round tests your thinking on a made-up product. Questions about your past work belong to the behavioral round, and the behavioral interview questions playbook covers those separately.

The three question shapes

Almost every prompt is one of three shapes. The structure for answering barely changes, but each shape has its own trap.

"Design a product for X"

Examples: design a product for gardeners, build a podcast product for Netflix, design something for people learning a language. The trap is treating the prompt as an invitation to pitch the first product you can picture. The whole point of the shape is watching you narrow an enormous space: which gardeners, trying to do what, stuck where. Spend your first minutes shrinking the problem, and don't propose anything until you've picked a user and a pain point.

"How would you improve Y"

Examples: improve Google Maps, improve a product you love, improve our product. The trap here is feature listing. Candidates who open with "I'd add dark mode and better search" have skipped the question of what the product is failing to do for whom. Shreyas Doshi's model of product work explains the difference interviewers hear instantly.

Impact, execution, optics. The player below is queued to 53:54, the exact moment Shreyas Doshi lays out the levels. Watch on YouTube.

"There are three levels of product work: impact, execution, and optics. Once you understand it, it explains a lot of what you see on product teams and organizations in general."
Shreyas Doshi, former product leader at Stripe, Twitter, and Google · Lenny's Podcast

A feature list is execution-level thinking. A strong improvement answer starts at the impact level: what outcome does this product exist to create, where is it falling short, and for which users. Only then do you propose changes, each one tied back to that outcome.

The ambiguous prompt

Some interviewers deliberately hand you something underspecified: "our engagement is flat, what would you do?" or "should we enter this market?" The vagueness is the test. Jiaona Zhang, who has hired PMs at Airbnb, WeWork, and Webflow, describes what she listens for when candidates hit ambiguity.

What a good answer to ambiguity sounds like. The player below is queued to 11:50, the exact moment Jiaona Zhang describes it. Watch on YouTube.

"Good answers are people who put structure and a way forward through the ambiguity, like that's what you look for. Like you want your PM to not just be like, oh no, we're swimming in ambiguity, but like actually put a path forward."
Jiaona Zhang, head of product at Webflow · Lenny's Podcast

She also looks for candidates who ask for inputs along the way instead of declaring one true path, because the odds of charting the right course on the first try are low in real product work too. Stating assumptions, proposing a plan, and inviting correction is exactly that behavior in miniature.

A structure that covers every signal

Erez coaches candidates to walk the five dimensions in a linear flow, and to say the plan out loud before starting. Here's the arc with rough timings for a 35-minute exercise:

1. Assumptions and game plan (3 to 5 minutes)

  • State two to four assumptions that shrink the scope: your role, the market or region, any constraint you're adopting.
  • Say how you'll spend the time, then check: "Does that structure work for you?"

2. Product motivation (3 to 5 minutes)

  • Say why the product exists, for users and for the company.
  • Compress it into one mission sentence. You'll steer by it for the rest of the interview.

3. Segmentation (8 to 10 minutes)

  • Name the players in the ecosystem, then split the user base into groups that differ in behavior and need, never just demographics.
  • Pick one segment using reach and how underserved they are, and say why out loud.

4. Problem identification (8 to 10 minutes)

  • Walk your chosen user's journey and pull out specific pain points, with context and emotional stakes.
  • Prioritize one problem by frequency and severity, and connect it to your mission.

5. Solution development (8 to 10 minutes)

  • Brainstorm two or three genuinely different approaches, then pick one using impact versus effort.
  • Scope a V1, say how users would discover it, name the biggest risk, and close the loop back to the mission.

The step candidates most want to skip is problem identification, because ideas are more fun than pain points. Scott Belsky, who built Behance and ran product for Adobe's creative tools, watched that exact mistake sink real teams for years.

Problems before solutions. The player below is queued to 8:56, the exact moment Scott Belsky says it. Watch on YouTube.

"The biggest mistakes that teams make is they become very passionate about a solution to a problem they're trying to solve as opposed to do everything they can to develop empathy for the customer that's suffering the problem. And oftentimes, the empathy gives you the solution."
Scott Belsky, chief product officer at Adobe, founder of Behance · Lenny's Podcast

In interview terms: if you describe your user's day in enough detail, the solution section gets much easier, and the interviewer gets to watch you work your way to an idea rather than defend one you brought with you.

A worked example: "How would you improve Spotify?"

Here's the structure applied, compressed. In the real interview each step gets the full timing above.

Assumptions and game plan. "I'll assume I'm a PM on consumer Spotify, focused on the mobile music experience rather than podcasts, in a mature market like the US. I'd like to spend a couple of minutes on what Spotify is trying to do, then segment listeners and pick one group, walk their listening day to find problems, and bring a couple of solutions down to a V1. Does that work for you?" Thirty seconds, and you've already banked most of the communication score.

Product motivation. Spotify makes money when people keep listening and keep subscribing, and people stay when the app reliably gives them the right audio for the moment they're in. Mission sentence: "help people get the right audio for every moment of their day."

Segmentation. The ecosystem has listeners, artists, and advertisers; you're focusing on listeners. Behavioral segments might be: curators who build playlists, lean-back listeners who press play on whatever's offered, and social listeners who treat music as something to share. Discovery for lean-back listeners is already well served by the algorithmic playlists, so pick social listeners: large reach, and the app treats sharing as an afterthought.

Problem identification. Walk a social listener's day: they hear a song that reminds them of a friend, send it, and then nothing happens, because the conversation is now in a chat app instead. They can't see what friends are into now, and reacting to a shared song means leaving Spotify entirely. Prioritize the reaction gap: people share often, and they rarely hear anything back.

Solution development. Three different approaches: reactions and short notes on shared songs inside the app, live listening rooms with friends, or a weekly blend recap you and a friend both see. Listening rooms are high effort and demand synchronous friends; the recap is low effort but slow feedback. Pick in-app reactions: high frequency, modest build. V1 is a react-and-reply layer on shared tracks, shown to the recipient when they play the song. Risk: it needs a critical mass of friends on Spotify, so seed it inside the existing Blend feature first. And that closes the loop: more moments where the right audio comes from someone who knows you.

None of these choices is the one right answer. A different segment and a different problem pass just as well, as long as every choice comes with a spoken reason.

What hiring managers screen for now

The rubric above hasn't changed much in years, but the bar around it has moved. In a panel conversation on The Skip, hiring leaders from Netflix, Rippling, and EvenUp agreed that most candidates are running a playbook that's about two years out of date. AI has made polished, well-structured documents trivial to produce, so the screens have shifted toward what a tool can't fake: depth of thought, the ability to make a complicated problem simple, and the judgment to question the prompt itself. The Netflix leader on the panel calls out one red flag in case exercises specifically: candidates who jump straight into answering without ever asking whether it's the right problem to solve.

That instinct, pausing to ask "is this the right problem?", is precisely what the assumptions step and the problem prioritization step give you room to show. The structure does more than help you remember the steps, because working through it forces you into the behavior senior interviewers are looking for.

How to practice in the weeks before

Erez is blunt about this: no one wings these interviews successfully. His own prep for the Meta loop took four intense weeks. The good news is that the practice compounds, because you're building the same muscle the job uses. Julie Zhuo's advice on developing product sense in the first place doubles as interview prep.

Where product sense comes from. The player below is queued to 44:06, the exact moment Julie Zhuo answers the question. Watch on YouTube.

"The number one advice that I always have for people when talking about product sense or product thinking is it's just really about observation and it's about curiosity. And you can start by first observing yourself... What was the new user experience like? What moment did it become clear to you what was going on? When were you confused? What did you do?"
Julie Zhuo, co-founder of Sundial, former VP of product design at Facebook · Lenny's Podcast

Zhuo's full method: observe your own reactions to every product you touch, then discuss products with other people and dissect the builders' decisions, then check your hypotheses against data wherever you can. Every one of those conversations is a low-stakes rehearsal of the interview.

For the interview-specific work, over two to three weeks:

  • Build an opening template. Write three or four placeholder assumptions you can adapt to any prompt, so the first two minutes never start from a blank page.
  • Rehearse 20 to 30 openings. Erez has candidates practice just the assumptions-and-game-plan opening across dozens of prompts, because a strong start settles your nerves for everything after it.
  • Drill problems, separately from solutions. Take five products and phrase pain points as "users struggle with Y because Z." If your sentence names a feature, you've written a solution and called it a problem, so rewrite it.
  • Map one persona's day per week. Sketch their day hour by hour and mark every touchpoint where a product could help. This builds the journey-mapping step until it's fast.
  • Do at least three full mocks out loud, timed. With a friend, a coach, or a tool, and record yourself. The clock is a big part of what makes this hard, and you only find that out by running the whole thing under time.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use a named framework like CIRCLES?

Use a structure, but own it. Interviewers at big tech companies have heard hundreds of candidates recite the same acronym, and naming it earns you nothing on its own. The five-part flow above hits every rubric dimension, so practice it until you can bend it to the prompt instead of marching through letters.

What if I know nothing about the domain in the prompt?

That's expected, and it's partly why the domain is chosen. You're being scored on structure, reasoning, and user empathy, never on gardening expertise. State an assumption ("I'll assume home gardeners in apartments, since that's who I can reason about"), and reason carefully from what you do know. Asking the interviewer a clarifying question or two is fine, but interrogating them for domain facts doesn't go over well.

What if the interviewer interrupts or challenges my choice?

Treat it as steering, and usually as help. "Are there other problems you'd consider?" usually means they want more breadth before you narrow. Pushback on your prioritization is an invitation to show your reasoning, and sometimes a test of whether you get defensive under pressure. Engage with curiosity, adjust if they've shown you something real, and keep your structure.

How is this different from a product strategy or analytical interview?

Product sense tests user-centered design thinking on an invented problem. Analytical or execution rounds test metrics, goal-setting, and diagnosis, and strategy rounds test market-level reasoning. Companies mix them differently, so ask your recruiter which rounds you're getting. Recruiters answer that question happily, and it costs you nothing to ask.

How long should I prepare?

If the interview is days away, focus on the opening template and two timed mocks. If you have a few weeks, run the full practice plan above. Erez spent four weeks preparing for his Meta loop while already working as a PM, and that's a reasonable benchmark for a competitive company.

The product sense brief on one page

Before you practice

  • Know the five scored dimensions: communication, motivation, segmentation, problems, solutions.
  • Write your opening template: three or four adaptable assumptions plus a game-plan sentence.
  • Pick 10 practice prompts across all three shapes: design for X, improve Y, ambiguous.

In every practice round

  • Say the game plan out loud and check in before starting.
  • Spend real minutes on segmentation and problems before any idea leaves your mouth.
  • Phrase problems as "users struggle with Y because Z," never as missing features.
  • Offer two or three genuinely different solutions, choose with impact versus effort, scope a V1.
  • Close the loop: connect the V1 back to the mission sentence.

In the interview

  • Pause to think before each section. Silence while thinking is fine, so announce it: "Give me a minute to gather my thoughts."
  • Waypoint every transition and check in with the interviewer.
  • Ask whether it's the right problem before you answer it.
  • Treat interruptions as steering, and adjust without losing the thread.
  • Keep improvement answers anchored to impact, with features as the last step.
