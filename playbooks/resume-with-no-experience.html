How to write a resume when you have no experience

You almost certainly have evidence. It's sitting in your class projects, your part-time job, and the club you ran, written up as activities instead of work.

12 min read

You're staring at a blank page thinking you have nothing to put on it. You haven't had a real job, your last summer was a retail shift, and the only projects you can name got a grade instead of a customer. So you either pad the page with adjectives or you don't send the resume at all.

This article covers what actually counts as experience, and how to lay out the page when you have no work history. It also covers writing project bullets that end in a result, why your part-time job matters more than you think, and what a resume genuinely can't fix. Read the sections about evidence first, because most people's problem is presentation rather than a real shortage of material.

The advice comes from Ethan Evans, a retired Amazon VP who spent 15 years there and reviewed real student and entry-level resumes live on his Level Up show, and from Nikhyl Singhal, former CPO of Credit Karma and VP of Product at Meta, who runs The Skip. Every quote plays the exact moment it was said.

"No experience" usually means "no job title"

When people say they have no experience, they almost always mean they've never held a full-time job with a title on a business card. That's a much smaller gap than it feels like. Hiring managers for entry-level roles already know you don't have five years of work behind you. What they're looking for is evidence that you've done something hard, finished it, and can say what came of it.

You have more of that evidence than you think. Singhal spent a whole episode of The Skip on how people actually land a first tech job, and the thing he keeps coming back to is what you did outside of class.

Working while studying. The player below is queued to 43:48, the exact moment Nikhyl Singhal says it. Watch on YouTube.

"One thing that really helped my career and helped almost all the leaders that I spoke with is they were trying to work while they were studying. That could be side hustle, it could be they were building things on their own, it could be internships, which I think are, and this is like maybe the craziest statement, is that your internships will matter far more than the name of the college you went to."
Nikhyl Singhal, former CPO of Credit Karma and VP of Product at Meta · The Skip

Before you write anything, spend 20 minutes making a list. Don't filter it yet, just get every candidate on the page.

Things that count as experience

  • Course projects. Especially capstones, group projects, and anything that shipped to a real user or got presented to people outside your class.
  • Internships and co-ops. Including short ones, unpaid ones, and ones where you feel you barely did anything.
  • Research and lab work. Anything with a professor, a dataset, or a published result.
  • Part-time and summer jobs. Retail, food service, warehouse, lifeguarding, tutoring, campus jobs, landscaping.
  • Clubs and student organizations. Especially anything where you held a role, ran an event, or handled money.
  • Volunteering. Charity drives, mentoring, community programs, anything you organized.
  • Side projects. Apps, repos, a store, a newsletter, a YouTube channel, a Discord community you moderated.
  • Competitions and hackathons. Case competitions, pitch contests, coding challenges, whether or not you won.
  • Freelance and gig work. Even one paid client counts, because someone paid you for a result.

Most people finish that list with 10 or 15 items and realize they had plenty of material all along. None of it had a job title attached, so you never wrote any of it down.

How to lay out the page with no work history

The standard resume order puts work experience first, which is exactly wrong for you. Evans's first note on a student resume was almost always about telling the reader who they're looking at before they start judging the content.

Education goes at the top. The player below is queued to 0:20, the exact moment Ethan Evans says it. Watch on YouTube.

"I might put that up front because it sets context to the fact that you're still in school. Because it immediately tells people, oh, you're a student. And that's okay, because it gives them the right expectations about what to look at in your experience and in your skills. So for students, I actually recommend putting your educational status straight up front."
Ethan Evans, retired Amazon VP · Level Up

That one move changes how everything below it gets read. Someone who already knows you're a junior in chemical engineering will forgive a thin work history, because they were never expecting a long one. Someone who hasn't figured that out yet spends the first ten seconds wondering why your jobs look so small.

Here's the order that works when you have no full-time roles:

One page, top to bottom

  • Name and contact. Email, phone, LinkedIn, and GitHub or a portfolio link if the work lives online.
  • Objective, two lines. What you're studying, when you graduate, and the specific role you want. Evans flagged a missing objective on nearly every student resume he reviewed, because without it nobody knows what you're applying for.
  • Education. School, degree, graduation date, GPA if it helps you, and a short relevant coursework line.
  • Experience. Internships, research, part-time jobs, all in one section, most recent first.
  • Projects. Three or four, each with bullets written like work.
  • Leadership. Club roles, teams you captained, charities you ran.
  • Skills. Languages, tools, certifications. This can go near the top or at the bottom; Evans has seen both work.

Two format warnings, because he raised them on almost every student resume. Give it white space. Reviewing a Waterloo student's page, he said the density itself was the problem: "It's about it being readable, and this is so dense the readability suffers." His reaction as the person holding a thousand resumes was blunt: "basically what that does for me as a reviewer is it irritates me, because now I have to do the work to sort of parse through your bad design." And spell out your acronyms. He hit "DECA" on that same resume and said "this is kind of meaningless to me," then suggested writing it as "DECA (high school marketing)" so a reader outside your world can follow.

There's an alternative to opening with education worth testing. Austin Belcak, who coaches job seekers at Cultivated Culture, opens the page with what he calls a Highlight Reel: a custom section titled for the target role, "Sales Summary" for an account executive application, holding three to five bullets. The section title does the positioning before anyone reads a line of the resume.

The Highlight Reel

  • One summary line. Who you are and what you're aiming at, with any number or social proof you have.
  • Two or three case-study bullets. Projects, class work, internships, each with a result.
  • One culture bullet. Volunteering, communities, initiatives you helped run.

The Dream Job System: the highlight reel. This one is audio only. The quote is at 2:30; the player starts from the top, or open it on Apple Podcasts cued to 2:30.

"Real world results always trump education. That's just the truth in pretty much any industry that you're in."
Austin Belcak, Cultivated Culture · The Dream Job System

Write project bullets the way you'd write a job

This is where most entry-level resumes lose. When you describe a course project the way your syllabus describes it, a reader sees homework. When you describe the same project the way you'd describe a job, a reader sees work, and almost all of that difference comes down to whether the bullet ends in a result.

Evans reviewed a Brown computer science student's resume that described building machine learning models with TensorFlow and annotating 200,000 video frames. Strong work. Every bullet stopped one step short.

Spell out the impact. The player below is queued to 1:48, the exact moment Ethan Evans says it. Watch on YouTube.

"Why? To what end? To what use? Who benefited? Tell me why. Because otherwise, it's good work you've done. And as a student, you're going to get a pass on this, but just get used to spelling out the business impact."
Ethan Evans, retired Amazon VP · Level Up

"You're going to get a pass on this" is worth reading twice. He's saying a student who skips results still gets looked at, and a student who includes them jumps the queue. It's the cheapest edge available to you.

The structure he teaches is STAR: situation, task, action, result. For a project, the situation is the class or the lab or the hackathon, the action is what you personally built, and the result is what changed because it existed. On a chemical engineering student's resume he pointed at exactly this pattern, praising a first bullet that trained 50 students on lab tools and saved senior process engineers 100 plus hours, then noting the next four bullets had no result at all: "You kind of started off well using STAR on your first thing and then you missed it on the next four."

Turning a project into a bullet

  • Activity version. "Built a web app for a class project using React and Firebase."
  • Work version. "Built a course scheduling web app in React and Firebase, adopted by roughly 400 students in the first semester and cut planning time from an afternoon to about ten minutes."
  • Activity version. "Analyzed a dataset of customer reviews for a machine learning course."
  • Work version. "Trained a sentiment classifier on 60,000 customer reviews, reaching 91 percent accuracy and beating the course baseline by 12 points."
  • Activity version. "Participated in a case competition."
  • Work version. "Led a four-person team to second place out of 40 in a regional case competition, presenting a market entry plan to a panel of six executives."

Notice that none of the work versions invent anything. The facts were already there. They just never got written down, because the person writing them didn't think a class project was allowed to have a result. The resume bullets playbook goes deeper on the sentence-level mechanics if you want more practice on this one skill.

When you can't find a business result, use what you have: how many people used it, how much time it saved, how it compared to a baseline, how many people you presented to, whether it's still running. Evans praised a student bullet about founding a charity drive on exactly this basis, saying "you've got what you accomplished and how you did it," and the only thing he wanted more of was whether it was still going.

Put numbers on everything you can

Numbers are the fastest way for a stranger to believe you. Evans reviewed a resume from a finance major graduating in May with no job lined up, liked most of it, then his chat pointed out the obvious.

A finance major with no numbers. The player below is queued to 2:55, the exact moment Ethan Evans reacts. Watch on YouTube.

"He's exactly right. As a finance major, where the hell are the numbers? You're supposed to be a numbers person. This should be filled with how big was this small food mart? Was it a 10,000 a day business? Was it a two dollar a business? Was it a million dollar turnover? You can put numbers in here."
Ethan Evans, retired Amazon VP · Level Up

He graded that resume a B for missing results generally, then dropped it to a C minus because a finance major with no numbers on the page undercuts the whole application. The lesson generalizes past finance: whatever you claim to be good at, the page should show you doing it.

You don't need audited figures, just honest scale. How many customers a shift, how many hours a week, how many people on the team, how many dollars in the budget, how many rows in the dataset, what it was before and after. Use a rounded number you can defend in an interview, and use it in place of an adjective.

When your own work has no dashboard, borrow one. Belcak's scripts for this work at any experience level: the club treasurer can ask "how did attendance move after the events I ran?", and the intern who made ad graphics can ask the marketing team how those ads performed against the average. Whoever consumed your work has numbers about it, and one conversation usually converts "helped with social media" into "designed posts that lifted engagement 40%."

While you're adding numbers, take out the inflated vocabulary. Evans hit a resume stacked with "executed, strategized and designed" and "hypothesized, conducted and analyzed A/B tests" and called it out: "you've like decided there's extra points for syllables, and it just sounds a little overblown." A plain verb with a real number after it does more for you than an impressive verb with nothing after it.

What to do with GPA and coursework

GPA is simple. Put it on if it's roughly 3.4 or above, or if it's the highest number you have and you're applying somewhere that screens on it. Leave it off otherwise, and let your projects carry the page. Reviewing a Brown student with a 3.7, Evans read the top corner and said most recruiters would think "okay, I'm interested, there's someone at a smart exclusive school doing pretty well." That's the entire job GPA does: buy you a few more seconds of attention.

Coursework deserves one line. Don't give it a whole section. List four to six courses that map to the job you want, and skip the intro classes everyone takes. "Relevant coursework: Distributed Systems, Machine Learning, Database Design, Operating Systems" tells a reader what you can be handed. A full transcript tells them nothing and eats space your projects need.

One thing to be careful with: don't write your resume for a future you. Evans caught a student listing a degree two years out while calling himself a junior and said "that a little bit makes it seem like you're lying about being a junior." He gave the fix too, which is the word "rising," as in rising junior. He was equally direct about a student who listed an internship that hadn't started yet: "I wouldn't put a job that you haven't done yet." If you want to mention it, label it as pending rather than dressing it up as work you've done.

Your retail job matters more than you think

The most common mistake students make is cutting the unrelated job. You worked 20 hours a week at a coffee shop through three semesters and you leave it off because it has nothing to do with software. That's a real loss, and hiring managers read it differently than you do.

Reviewing a grad student's cloud computing resume, Evans got to a line about being a crew leader for a mowing company and reacted to it more warmly than to any of the technical work.

The hard job on the resume. The player below is queued to 4:04, the exact moment Ethan Evans says it. Watch on YouTube.

"Being able to put in there that you work this hard job goes to the point of grit, determination. And you probably listened to one of my streams where I said I like that, because I hear someone I know who knows how to work. Like if you really were a crew leader for mowing contractors for 13 flipping years and now you're a grad student in comp sci, good on you."
Ethan Evans, retired Amazon VP · Level Up

Keep the job. Then write it like work, with the same rules as everything else. A shift at a restaurant has volume, speed, money, training, and scheduling in it, and all of those are countable.

Rewriting an unrelated job

  • Before. "Cashier. Took orders and handled the register."
  • After. "Ran register and drive-through during peak shifts averaging 300 customers, and trained six new hires on the point of sale system."
  • Before. "Campus tour guide."
  • After. "Led weekly campus tours for groups of 20 to 30 prospective students and families, and rewrote the tour script the admissions office now uses."
  • Before. "Worked at a warehouse over the summer."
  • After. "Picked and packed roughly 400 orders per shift, and proposed a bin layout change that cut average pick time by about a minute."

These jobs do two more things for you. They fill a gap that would otherwise look like nothing happened, and they hand you behavioral interview stories about conflict, pressure, and difficult customers that are usually better than anything from a group project. The behavioral interview questions playbook covers how to build those into answers.

What a resume can't fix

Be honest with yourself about the ceiling here. A great entry-level resume gets you into a pile with hundreds of similar ones. If you have no relevant projects at all, no amount of editing covers that, and the document can't introduce you to anyone inside the company. Evans is direct about how little it's supposed to do.

What the resume is actually for. The player below is queued to 8:02, the exact moment Ethan Evans says it. Watch on YouTube.

"I think with a resume you want a resume that is good enough to get the job done without thinking that resumes are going to get you the job. Resumes are going to get you a phone screen for the job maybe, or an interview, and that's all they're supposed to do. So having the most perfect beautiful resume in the world, not needed. Having a really effective tool for the job, needed."
Ethan Evans, retired Amazon VP · Level Up

He gave that student a ten to thirty minute budget for the remaining edits and told him to go get the job. Take the same deal. Once the page is good, the extra hours belong somewhere else, and for an entry-level search that somewhere is building evidence and talking to people.

Singhal makes the same argument from the other side, about what separates people who land a first job from people who stay stuck.

Grit and hustle after graduation. The player below is queued to 1:03:21, the exact moment Nikhyl Singhal says it. Watch on YouTube.

"I really love this point that grit and hustle are not necessarily valued in academics, but are essential now. Maybe the overarching thing is things are not as different as they may seem. Do not blame the environment. The environment is tricky, generally speaking. It's always hard to get those first jobs. So don't be a victim. Don't think the environment is out to get you. You can show initiative today."
Nikhyl Singhal, former CPO of Credit Karma and VP of Product at Meta · The Skip

Two things to do with that. First, keep building evidence while you apply, because every new project is a new line and a new thing to talk about. Second, stop relying on the portal. A referral or a short conversation with someone on the team does more for an entry-level application than any rewrite, and the foot in the door playbook covers how to build that path. Then tailor what you send using the tailoring playbook, so the version that arrives points at the job in front of it.

Entry-level mistakes worth avoiding

These came up over and over in Evans's student reviews.

The recurring ones

  • No objective. He flagged this on almost every student resume. Two lines saying what you're studying and what you want.
  • Bullets that stop before the result. "Resumes tend to leave the story hanging," as he put it. Finish the sentence.
  • Going past one page. With no full-time roles, one page is the right length. Cut a project before you add a second page.
  • Cramming to fit. A dense page gets skimmed worse than a shorter one. Trim a bullet and take the white space.
  • Acronyms and inside references. Club names, campus programs, and lab abbreviations mean nothing to a stranger. Expand them in parentheses.
  • Big words instead of facts. Plain verbs, real numbers.
  • Skills lists with no levels. If you list ten languages, mark which ones you'd want to be tested on.
  • Hobbies taking real estate. He cut a hobby section that was crowding out work history. Keep one line at most, and only if it's relevant or genuinely interesting.
  • Listing work you haven't done. Future internships, degrees not yet earned, titles you're about to have.
  • Sending the same file everywhere. When a viewer described one resume as spray and pray rather than targeting a certain job, Evans agreed and told the student to customize. Tailor for the jobs you actually want.

Frequently asked questions

Should my resume be one page?

Yes. With no full-time roles, everything you have fits on one page, and a second page signals padding. If you're running long, cut your weakest project rather than shrinking the margins.

Do I include my GPA?

Include it if it's around 3.4 or higher, or if a specific employer asks for it. Below that, leave it off and let projects and internships do the work. Nobody assumes a missing GPA is a good one, but a mediocre number sitting at the top of the page does you more damage than leaving it out.

What if I've genuinely never had a job?

Then your experience section is projects, coursework, and volunteering, and you label it "Projects and Experience" rather than pretending otherwise. Three well-written projects with real outcomes beat one weak internship. Then go make a fourth: a small tool, a freelance client, a volunteer role with a measurable result. Two months of deliberate effort is enough to change what's on the page.

Should I list a job I quit after two weeks?

Skip it. It's not long enough to have produced anything you can write a bullet about, and it invites a question that costs you more than the line gains.

Can I use a resume template with columns and colors?

A little color and a photo are fine, and Evans has told students to consider both. What matters more is readability: clear sections, real gaps between them, and more space above a heading than between two bullets. If the template creates dense columns, it's working against you.

How do I write about a group project without overstating my role?

Name the team size and your specific piece. "One of four engineers; owned the authentication flow and the deployment pipeline" is honest and more credible than claiming the whole thing. Interviewers ask what you personally did, so write the bullet you'd want to answer questions about.

Does anyone actually check whether projects are real?

Interviewers ask about them in detail, which amounts to the same thing. Link the repo or the live site where you can, and only list projects you can walk through for five minutes.

The method on one page

Before you write

  • List everything: projects, internships, part-time jobs, clubs, volunteering, side projects, competitions.
  • For each one, write down what changed because you did it, even roughly.
  • Pick the six to eight items with the clearest outcomes.

Building the page

  • Contact, then a two-line objective, then education with GPA and a coursework line.
  • Experience section combining internships, research, and part-time jobs.
  • Three or four projects, each bullet ending in a result.
  • Leadership and skills, then stop at one page.

Editing

  • Every bullet: what you did, how, and what came of it.
  • Add a number to anything you can count honestly.
  • Swap inflated verbs for plain ones, and expand every acronym.
  • Trim until there's real white space between sections.

After the page is good

  • Give it another 30 minutes at most, then start applying.
  • Keep building projects while you apply, since each one adds a line.
  • Find one person inside each company you care about, instead of sending more applications.
  • Practice talking through your top three projects out loud before any interview.
