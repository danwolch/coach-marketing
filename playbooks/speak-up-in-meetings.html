Meeting Confidence

How to speak up in meetings when you are quiet or introverted

Prepare one useful contribution, get in early, and use questions when you need more time to think.

11 min read

You have a point, but the conversation moves on while you're still working out how to say it. By the time your answer feels ready, someone's changed the topic. Then you leave annoyed, because you know you could have helped.

This article covers how to prepare, how to get into the conversation, what to do when someone interrupts you, and how to follow up if you process things slowly. Start by picking one meeting this week and one contribution you want to make in it.

The advice comes from Deb Liu, CEO of Ancestry and former VP at Facebook; Paige Costello, a product leader who has worked at Asana, Intercom, and Intuit; and Jules Walter, formerly a product leader at Slack, YouTube, and Google.

Judge yourself on what you add

Ask whether what you said made the work better. It doesn't matter how often the most talkative person speaks. Your job is to get useful information or a good question in front of the group while they can still use it.

Choose one of these jobs before the meeting:

  • state the customer fact others may not know;
  • ask which outcome or decision the discussion is serving;
  • name a risk before the group commits;
  • summarize where people agree and what remains open;
  • recommend the next step and owner.

One useful contribution is a better goal than "be more vocal," because you can actually prepare for it and tell afterward whether you did it.

Senior people miss work they never hear you describe

Deb Liu tells the story of an excellent product manager whose work was hard for senior colleagues to see. She processed questions slowly, and the discussion often moved on before she spoke.

"The workplace is really favoring people who can speak up... I asked her once, I said, 'I noticed that you never answer questions when we do these presentations.' She's like, 'Yeah, because I'm a processor. And by the time I process, I feel like the conversation has moved on.'"
Deb Liu, CEO of Ancestry and former VP of Product at Facebook · Lenny's Podcast

You can stay quiet by nature and still have a dependable way to say what you know, especially when a decision affects your work or your customers.

Prepare one sentence before the meeting

Read the agenda and complete one prompt:

Your prepared contribution

  • "The point I want people to know is..."
  • "The risk I want checked before the decision is..."
  • "The question that would clarify this discussion is..."
  • "The recommendation I can support with evidence is..."

Add one example or number. Don't write a whole paragraph, because then you'll sit there waiting for an opening big enough to deliver all of it. A single sentence fits almost anywhere.

Speak once in the first ten minutes

Your first contribution can be small. Ask a clarifying question, confirm the outcome, or add one relevant fact. Speaking early makes it easier to speak again, because you've already heard your own voice in the conversation.

How to get in early

  • "Before we compare options, can we confirm whether the date or the full scope is the priority?"
  • "One customer fact that may matter here is that all three enterprise accounts asked for audit export."
  • "I agree with the outcome. I want to test one assumption in the proposed route."

If you wait until your answer is fully formed, faster talkers will keep handing you new things to process. Say the part you already know, and add more only if the group needs it.

Questions count as contributions

A good question can expose an assumption, or slow down a group that's deciding faster than the evidence supports. Paige Costello points out that asking for clarification can make you look confident, because it shows you're willing to admit what you don't understand.

Asking when you don't understand. The player below is queued to 27:02, the exact moment Paige Costello explains it. Watch on YouTube.

"Real confidence is often conveyed by being willing to ask the question or to say, 'I don't know what you mean by that. Can you say that again?'"
Paige Costello, product leader formerly at Asana, Intercom, and Intuit · Lenny's Podcast

Avoid questions that hide your view. "Have we considered customers?" gives the group little to work with. Try: "The three customers I spoke with need export controls. How are we weighing that evidence against the faster option?"

Say you're still thinking

If you go quiet, people can't tell what you're thinking, and some of them will take that as not caring. Jules Walter learned that when he went quiet under stress, a mentor assumed he was disengaged. He started saying out loud that he was still thinking, and talking through enough of it to keep people with him.

Going quiet under stress. The player below is queued to 23:49, the exact moment Jules Walter describes the habit. Watch on YouTube.

"When I'm under stress, for example, I tend to withdraw and not say anything. So then you might think I'm disengaged... once you know these patterns, you know, okay, I'm stressed right now, but I need to say something so that people see that I'm actually interested in this problem. I'm just thinking through it."
Jules Walter, product leader formerly at Slack, YouTube, and Google · Lenny's Podcast

If you need a second, say so out loud. Three sentences that buy you one without giving up the floor:

Ways to ask for a moment

  • "I'm thinking through the dependency. Give me a moment."
  • "My first reaction is that the date is possible. I want to check the staffing assumption before I say yes."
  • "I have a view, but I need to separate the customer evidence from my preference."

One sentence like that buys you a pause without dropping out of the conversation. The thought-organizing playbook has ways to structure what you say after the pause.

Reclaim the floor after an interruption

Don't race the other person. Stop, let the overlap clear, and come back with a short sentence:

When someone interrupts you

  • "I want to finish the risk, then I'd like your reaction."
  • "There is one more fact that changes my recommendation."
  • "I'll complete this sentence, then please go ahead."

When the group has already moved on

  • "I want to go back to the decision for 30 seconds. I didn't finish the part about the customer impact."
  • "Before we close, there is one unresolved dependency I need everyone to know."

If the same person keeps interrupting you, raise it with them privately and bring examples. Ask whoever runs the meeting to use a speaker queue or go around the group, if the meeting usually favors whoever talks fastest.

Treat speaking up as a skill you can practice

Deb Liu argues that calling yourself bad at speaking up writes off any chance of getting better at it. She ties the skill to the everyday work of making the case for your product and your team.

Speaking up as a skill. The player below is queued to 38:14, the exact moment Deb Liu says it. Watch on YouTube.

"I think we do a disservice when we say we're not good at speaking up, because it's a skill like any other... Every day you're actually building credibility for your team, getting more resources, getting more people to talk about your product inside your company."
Deb Liu, CEO of Ancestry and former VP of Product at Facebook · Lenny's Podcast

Work up to it:

  1. Ask one prepared question in a familiar meeting.
  2. Give one 30-second update without reading.
  3. State a different view with one piece of evidence.
  4. Lead one agenda item and summarize the decision.

If nerves are the main thing stopping you, try the exercises in the meeting-confidence playbook.

Use writing before and after the meeting

Send a short note before the meeting when the topic is complex: "I have included the customer evidence and my recommendation in the document. I'd like five minutes to test the tradeoff." That way people have read your thinking before anyone starts talking over each other.

Follow up when you didn't find an opening: "I didn't raise this clearly in the meeting. The dependency on legal review changes my recommendation. Here are the facts and the decision I think we should reopen." Use writing to add to what you say in meetings. It shouldn't permanently replace speaking up while a decision is still open.

If you lead the meeting, make participation easier

Deb Liu describes offline voting, written comments, and asking each person for a view. All of those cut down the advantage that goes to whoever speaks first.

  • Send the decision question before the meeting.
  • Give everyone two quiet minutes to write before discussion.
  • Ask for views before the most senior person speaks.
  • Use a round when the decision requires every function.
  • Invite written follow-up and state when the decision closes.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I speak in a meeting?

There's no useful universal number. Speak when you have information other people don't, when you see a risk in the decision, when you can clear something up, or when you need to ask for something.

What if I have nothing new to add?

You don't need to repeat someone else's point just to be seen. You can back it up briefly, ask a clarifying question, or stay quiet and follow through on whatever gets decided.

How do I speak up with senior leaders present?

Prepare your conclusion and one fact that backs it up, and keep what you say tied to the decision. When you're missing context, ask for it instead of pretending to know more than you do.

What if my ideas are ignored until someone else repeats them?

Say it calmly: "Yes, that's the customer risk I raised earlier. I agree with Sam's recommendation to test it." Talk to your manager if it keeps happening and it's affecting how your work is seen.

The one-week speaking-up plan

Before the meeting

  • Choose one contribution: fact, question, risk, summary, or recommendation.
  • Write the first sentence and one supporting example.
  • Send anything complicated ahead of time.

During the meeting

  • Speak once in the first ten minutes.
  • Say when you need a moment to process.
  • Use a question to expose the decision or assumption.
  • Finish an interrupted point with one calm sentence.

After the meeting

  • Send relevant evidence or the point you did not finish.
  • Note what made it easier or harder to jump in.
  • Try something slightly harder next time.
