Job Search

How to job search while you're still employed

Most job search advice assumes you have all day. Here's the version for someone with a full calendar, a manager, and a reputation to protect.

13 min read

You have maybe five hours a week for this, and two of them are at night when you're tired. You can't take calls from your desk, you can't put "open to work" on LinkedIn, and you can't disappear for a four-hour onsite without someone noticing. So you open a job board, apply to nine things in a burst of energy on Sunday, hear nothing, and repeat that for a couple of months.

This article covers what to change when you're searching from inside a job: how to pick fewer targets, and how much time to actually spend each week. It also covers keeping the search quiet, scheduling interviews without lying, and what to do about the offer, the counteroffer, and the resignation at the end. Read the sections you need now and come back for the later ones when you get there, because the whole thing can run for months.

The advice comes from Phyl Terry, whose book Never Search Alone has run thousands of candidates through job search councils; Lauren Ipsen, an executive recruiter at Daversa Partners who has placed around 85 executives; Jacob Warwick, who negotiates compensation for senior tech executives; and Anthony Klotz, the management professor who coined the phrase "the Great Resignation" and studies how people quit.

Decide what you want before you start applying

Volume works as a strategy when you're unemployed and you have the hours to back it up. On five hours a week it quietly wrecks the search. Twenty applications you wrote at 11 p.m. produce a handful of screens you're not prepared for, scheduled at times you can't take, for jobs you don't want. Then you're exhausted and no further along.

Terry's answer is candidate market fit, his term for a target narrow enough to say in one sentence: level, function, company stage, industry, location. His example is "a director of product role in a healthcare Series B startup in San Francisco." People resist it because it sounds like it rules opportunities out, when really the people who want to help you can only help if they know what to look for. We go through how to build that statement in how to position yourself for a job.

Write yours down before you apply to anything, and underneath it write what you'd actually leave your current job for. If you can't name something specific, you probably won't like the offers you get, and you'll spend three months finding that out the slow way.

Four things to write down first

  • The level and function you're targeting, in one sentence.
  • The company stage and industry where your experience is worth the most.
  • The two or three things you want that your current job can't give you.
  • The things you'd refuse: a commute, a title cut, a pay cut, a relocation.

Give the search a weekly budget you can keep

Terry's community sorts people into two groups when they sign up, and the distinction is the whole point of this article.

Slow seekers and fast seekers. The player below is queued to 10:43, the exact moment Phyl Terry explains the split. Watch on YouTube.

"When you apply, we ask first of all, are you in a job and looking, or are you out of work? Because we separate those two, because they have different cadences. If you're in a job and looking, we call you a slow seeker, because you have a full-time job and you can't work as quickly. If you're out of work and looking, we call you a fast seeker, and we put you in different groups."
Phyl Terry, author of Never Search Alone · Lenny's Podcast

Being a slow seeker is just the real pace of your search, and you'll do better planning around it than pretending you'll find ten hours a week you don't have. Four to six hours is a realistic budget, and it goes further if you spend it on conversations rather than applications.

A five-hour week

  • 90 minutes, one weekend block: research two or three companies properly and write one tailored application. One.
  • Two lunch hours: two conversations with people who know your target market. Ask what they're seeing and what they think you're a fit for.
  • 30 minutes midweek: follow-ups, replies to recruiters, updating your notes on where each process stands.
  • 60 minutes: prep for whatever interview is actually on your calendar this week.

Energy usually runs out before the hours do. Terry argues that your emotional state is the main thing to manage in a job search, and that gets harder when the search sits on top of a full week of work.

The emotional balance sheet. The player below is queued to 13:51, the exact moment Phyl Terry says it. Watch on YouTube.

"Most people think, what's the most important thing to manage during your job search? They think, well, maybe it's their resume or LinkedIn profile or their ability to network, or candidate market fit, a concept I introduce. All those things are really important, but the most important thing to manage is your emotional balance. I talk about your emotional balance sheet, and for many job seekers, they have more liabilities than assets. They have more fear and anxiety. They feel demoralized."
Phyl Terry, author of Never Search Alone · Lenny's Podcast

Terry's fix is a job search council, a small group of people going through the same thing who meet regularly and hold each other accountable. If that's more structure than you want, get one person. Someone who knows you're looking, hears about each interview, and asks you next week whether you did the thing you said you'd do. A slow search feels much worse when nobody else knows it's happening.

Keep the search quiet without lying about it

Keeping a search confidential mostly comes down to controlling where the information travels. A few settings and habits do most of the work.

The confidentiality checklist

  • LinkedIn: turn off "share profile changes with your network" before you touch your profile, and use the recruiter-only version of "open to work" rather than the green banner.
  • Devices and accounts: personal laptop, personal phone, personal email. Never your work Slack, work calendar, or a company printer.
  • Calendar: your work calendar is often visible to the whole company. Block time as "busy" or "personal", never "interview".
  • References: use people who've already left, and ask each one directly to keep it confidential. Tell the company your current manager can be a reference only after you've accepted.
  • Who you tell: your one accountability person, your references, and nobody at your current company. Not the friendly colleague. Not your skip-level. Not the person you'd trust with anything.
  • Recruiters: say in the first message that you're employed and the search is confidential. Good ones handle this every day and will not contact your company.

The one place people get creative and shouldn't is dates and titles. Recruiters check, and Ipsen's tolerance for fuzziness is low.

Straight facts on dates and titles. The player below is queued to 51:59, the exact moment Lauren Ipsen names her red flag. Watch on YouTube.

"You should always be honest about, like, when you started, when you left a company. If I see that someone still has 'present' on their LinkedIn but they've been gone for two years, and they kind of are acting like they're still there, that doesn't feel good. I just want people to give you the truth, the straight facts, so that you can support them and tell their story and represent them in a way that makes you feel like you have all the details."
Lauren Ipsen, executive recruiter and partner at Daversa Partners · Lenny's Podcast

You can be discreet without being dishonest. Declining to volunteer that you're interviewing is fine. Inventing a title, stretching an end date, or telling your manager you're committed to a two-year roadmap you plan to leave in six weeks is a different thing, and people usually find out.

Schedule interviews around a job you still have

This is where most employed searches fall apart. A recruiter offers Wednesday at 2 p.m., you have a team meeting, you say yes anyway, and now you're inventing a dentist appointment. Do that four times and you've built a small pile of lies for a job you might not get.

The alternative is telling the recruiter your constraints on day one, in plain language, before scheduling starts. Recruiters schedule around employed candidates constantly. What they can't work around is a candidate who accepts every slot and then reschedules.

What to send the recruiter up front

"Quick note on logistics: I'm currently employed and keeping this confidential, so my availability is narrow. I can do 8 to 9 a.m., 12 to 1 p.m., or after 5 p.m. on most days, and I can usually do a longer block on a Friday with a week's notice. If the process needs a half-day onsite, tell me now and I'll plan a day off around it."

For the time you do have to take, use PTO. A half day of vacation for a final round is clean and needs no explanation. Wrapping an onsite around an existing day off, a Friday, or the day before a holiday keeps it unremarkable.

You'll also feel pressure to move faster than your calendar allows. Warwick's advice is to push back on it, and his reasoning applies to scheduling as much as to money.

Slowing the process down on purpose. The player below is queued to 15:16, the exact moment Jacob Warwick says it. Watch on YouTube.

"Too hasty equals risk. So as you slow down, oftentimes we want to take a couple of days to respond, not to be a jerk or belligerent or to manufacture some fake urgency, but it shows a little scarcity and thought process to your time, and we get to calculate and collect information through the process."
Jacob Warwick, executive compensation negotiator · Lenny's Podcast

Two more rules worth holding. Ask the recruiter to batch interviews into one block rather than spreading five conversations across three weeks of stolen lunch hours, and ask for at least three business days of notice so you can prep instead of walking into a call cold. If a process demands same-day availability again and again, that tells you something about how they'd treat your time as an employee.

Meanwhile, keep your current work visible. Ship what you said you'd ship and don't let your output dip in the exact month you might need a reference.

What to do if your employer finds out

Sometimes it leaks. A recruiter calls a mutual contact, someone spots you in a lobby, a colleague sees a calendar block. If your manager asks you directly, you have three options and only one of them is bad.

If your manager asks whether you're looking

  • Honest and open: "I've been talking to a couple of companies. Nothing is decided. I'd rather tell you that than have you hear it somewhere else." Use this when you trust your manager and you'd genuinely consider staying.
  • Honest and bounded: "I've taken a couple of calls. I'm not planning to go anywhere right now, and if that changes you'll hear it from me first." True, and it doesn't hand over your timeline.
  • Turn it into the real conversation: "I have been thinking about what's next for me, and I'd rather talk about that with you than around you. Can we spend a one-on-one on where this role goes in the next year?"
  • What not to do: flatly deny it. If it later turns out you were interviewing that week, you've spent trust you'll need for the reference and the notice period.

Then be realistic about what follows. In most companies nothing happens. In some, you quietly stop getting the interesting projects. If you're in an at-will state and your relationship with your manager is already bad, assume the timeline just got shorter and move your search up accordingly.

Your current job gives you more room to negotiate

Here's the part people underuse. A paycheck you already have is a strong position to negotiate from, because you can say no and nothing bad happens to you.

Why employed candidates negotiate better. The player below is queued to 36:25, the exact moment Jacob Warwick explains it. Watch on YouTube.

"Understand that once you're employed and somebody's asking you and your needs are already getting met, why do you need to get pushed around? This is one reason that it's easier to find a job when you have a job. Now, I think this is a myth for what it's worth, but this is easier because naturally your needs are getting met financially to a degree. So naturally you negotiate more confident."
Jacob Warwick, executive compensation negotiator · Lenny's Podcast

Warwick goes on to describe the opposite case: someone out of work for a year, whose identity is tied to a title they no longer have, giving in to demands because they want the discomfort to end. You're not in that position. Use it.

The ethical line here is simple: say true things. "I'm currently employed, so I'm being selective and I'm not in a rush" is both true and useful to you. "I have a competing offer at 40% more" when you don't is a lie a recruiter can often check, and it ends the process when it unravels. If you do have another live process, say so with real dates. The salary negotiation scripts cover the wording once numbers are on the table.

Terry's framing of the same idea is that once you reach the interview and offer stage, you should stop behaving like a supplicant.

Play to win. The player below is queued to 49:34, the exact moment Phyl Terry says it. Watch on YouTube.

"Here's the thing: when you start to interview and negotiate, you've got to be in charge. People think, when I talk, this is collaborative coaching, I want you to play to win, not not to lose. Now when people hear me say that, they translate it in their brains into, oh, you're saying that I've got to be a ruthless negotiator. Anyone who knows me, like, ruthless is just not how I am."
Phyl Terry, author of Never Search Alone · Lenny's Podcast

While all this is running, keep the other processes alive. A second live conversation is what keeps you from accepting a bad number out of fear that this is the only thing on the table. If a recruiter does disappear on you, the follow-up timeline for recruiter silence has the cadence and the exact messages.

Compare the offer against staying

Employed candidates have one option unemployed candidates don't: staying. Treat it as a real choice and compare it on the same terms as the offer, rather than on how annoyed you felt this week.

Score both jobs on the same six things

  • Total compensation: base, bonus, equity value and vesting schedule, plus what you'd forfeit by leaving before a vest or bonus date.
  • The work itself: what you'd spend Tuesday morning doing in each job.
  • The manager: usually the biggest factor in whether you're happy a year from now. What do you actually know about the new one?
  • Growth: the next role after this one, and whether either job gets you there.
  • Risk: runway, recent layoffs, whether the team you'd join was reorganized twice last year.
  • Life: commute, travel, hours, flexibility you currently have and would lose.

Do this before you have the offer in hand, because the week an offer arrives is the worst week to think clearly. Write down what would make you say yes and what would make you say no, then check the offer against the note you wrote when you weren't excited.

Handling a counteroffer from your current employer

If you resign and you're any good, there's a reasonable chance your employer counters, because replacing you is expensive and slow. Klotz, who studies how people quit, tells Adam Grant that a counteroffer is one of the specific things to prepare for before you have the conversation.

"You can also add a whole lot of practice in, and you just threw out some scenarios that you definitely want to be prepared for. What happens if they make a counter offer? Being prepared for all the different reactions that your boss may have, from crying to laughing and being happy about you leaving, to being really angry."
Anthony Klotz, management professor at Texas A&M · WorkLife with Adam Grant (17:28, audio)

Decide your answer before you resign, rather than in the moment when someone is telling you how much they value you. The useful question is whether the counter fixes the reason you looked. If you left over pay, they match it, and the rest of the job was fine, staying can be the right call. If you left because of your manager or where the company is going, money doesn't touch any of that, and you'll be having this conversation again next year with less goodwill.

Questions to answer before the counteroffer arrives

  • What was the actual reason I started looking? Would this counter change it?
  • Why did it take a resignation for them to offer this?
  • What am I giving up with the company I'd be turning down, and would they take my call again?
  • Am I staying because I want to, or because saying no to my manager's face is hard?

If you decline it, decline it once and clearly: "I appreciate it, and I've made my decision. This isn't about the money." Reopening the negotiation after you've said no leaves everyone worse off.

How to resign without damaging the relationship

You'll want references from these people for the rest of your career, and Ipsen's description of how that works should be enough motivation on its own.

Leave the place better than you found it. The player below is queued to 44:21, the exact moment Lauren Ipsen explains why exits follow you. Watch on YouTube.

"Whether you are exploratory, opportunistic, not looking at all, people are constantly calling each other, and the world of tech is so, so, so small. And no matter how fired up you were leaving a company, or what went sideways, it is so critical that you try to leave the place better than you found it, and do so as seamlessly as possible, because you want people to have nothing but positive to say."
Lauren Ipsen, executive recruiter and partner at Daversa Partners · Lenny's Podcast

Klotz's research points at a specific mistake people make here. When you decide to quit, the power balance you've lived with flips, and some people spend that on a dramatic exit they later regret. The other common version is quieter and just as damaging: resigning by email because it's easier.

WorkLife with Adam Grant: The Not-So-Great Resignation. This one is audio only. The quotes are at 17:28 and 16:27; the player starts from the top, or open it on Apple Podcasts cued to 17:28.

"I've talked to lots of individuals that say, how do I write a good resignation email? How do I write a good resignation text message? And my research suggests that those are typically construed by managers as very avoidant styles of resigning, and can be pretty insulting to managers. And then you get your resignation started on the wrong foot. You'll just gain all the respect and positive momentum if you just have that face to face conversation right from the start."
Anthony Klotz, management professor at Texas A&M · WorkLife with Adam Grant (16:27, audio)

He also has a good way to choose a notice period. Rather than defaulting to two weeks, ask how you'd resign in a way that causes the least disruption to the people you're leaving behind, then set the date around the projects and handovers that actually need you.

The resignation sequence

  • Sign first. Written offer, start date, everything you negotiated in the document. Then resign.
  • Tell your manager first, live. Video or in person, and before anyone else in the company hears it.
  • Keep it short and warm. "I've accepted a role somewhere else. My last day would be the 28th. I want to make this handover as clean as possible."
  • Send the written note after the conversation, confirming the last day. That's what the email is for.
  • Agree the story. Ask your manager how they'd like to tell the team, and let them go first.
  • Do the handover properly. Documentation, open threads, introductions. This is the part people remember.
  • Skip the venting. The exit interview is not the place to settle anything, and the world is small.

Frequently asked questions

Should I tell my boss I'm looking?

Usually not, and not until you have a signed offer. The exception is a manager you genuinely trust who could fix the reason you're looking. Then it's better framed as a conversation about your growth than as an announcement that you're interviewing.

How many applications should I have running at once?

Three to five live processes is plenty for an employed candidate, and it's about the most you can prepare for properly on five hours a week. Adding a sixth usually means preparing worse for all of them.

What do I say when they ask why I'm leaving?

Something true and forward-looking. "I've learned a lot here and I'm looking for more scope in [specific area], which isn't available on my current team." Don't criticize your employer, and don't pretend everything is perfect either.

Can I use my current manager as a reference?

Not during the process. Offer former managers and colleagues who've left, and tell the company your current manager is available after you've accepted. Any competent recruiter understands this.

How long should this take?

Longer than an unemployed search, often three to six months, and longer at senior levels. Plan for that at the start, so a quiet month doesn't convince you the search has failed.

What if I get an interview I can't prepare for?

Ask to move it. If you can't, use the time you have deliberately rather than reading everything about the company. The 24-hour interview prep plan is built for exactly this.

The plan on one page

Before you apply to anything

  • Write your target in one sentence: level, function, company stage, industry, location.
  • Name what you want that your current job can't give you.
  • Set the weekly hours you can actually spend, and pick one person who'll hold you to them.
  • Fix your LinkedIn settings before you touch your profile.

While the search runs

  • Use your own devices and your own email. Nothing on a work account.
  • Tell recruiters your constraints in the first message, then hold the line on notice and batching.
  • Use PTO for onsites rather than inventing appointments.
  • Keep your current work visible and your commitments met.
  • Keep three to five processes live so no single one owns your leverage.

Offer, counteroffer, exit

  • Score the offer and staying on the same six criteria, and write your answer down before the offer arrives.
  • Negotiate from the fact that you have a job, using only true statements.
  • Decide your counteroffer answer before you resign.
  • Sign, then resign in a live conversation with your manager first.
  • Set the notice period around the handover, and do the handover well.
