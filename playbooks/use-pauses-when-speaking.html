How to use pauses when speaking

Where to pause, how long to hold it, and how to keep the silence from feeling awkward.

11 min read

You stop talking for a second and the silence feels enormous. It feels like everyone can tell you lost your place, so you rush to fill the gap with "so" or "um" and hurry into the next sentence. The people listening usually hear something much less dramatic: a person finishing one thought before starting the next.

This article covers the four pauses worth using, where each one goes, and scripts for keeping your pause when the meeting moves fast. Try one pause at a time in low-stakes meetings before you rely on it in a high-stakes one.

The advice comes from Tristan de Montebello, co-founder of the speaking school Ultraspeaking, and Matt Abrahams, who has taught strategic communication at Stanford's Graduate School of Business for 25 years and hosts the podcast Think Fast, Talk Smart.

Choose the pause for the job

  • Thinking pause: before an answer, so you can pick your first sentence.
  • Boundary pause: between two points, so listeners can tell them apart.
  • Emphasis pause: right before or after the line you want people to remember.
  • Invitation pause: after you ask a question, so someone has room to answer.

Most awkward pauses are awkward because the speaker treats them as a mistake. You trail off mid-phrase, look alarmed, or apologize, and everyone takes the cue that something went wrong. A pause after a complete phrase, with your posture settled, comes across as deliberate.

Pause before your first word

The pause with the biggest payoff happens before you say anything. It stops a half-formed opening from turning into a long, wandering answer. Tristan de Montebello does this on camera, mid-interview, without announcing it.

Watch on YouTube.

Three minutes later, Lenny stops the interview to point it out: "I love that you actually demoed that in the way you answered this question, where you took time to get into that state" (15:16 in the episode). Nobody watching thought the silence was a failure. De Montebello's explanation for why we avoid the pause anyway: until you've proven to yourself that you can take a moment to gather your thoughts without everything unraveling, you'll "go for safety" and keep talking (14:35).

Use the beat to choose your conclusion. You don't need to draft the whole answer in your head first; one complete opening sentence is enough: "Yes, we can keep the date if we reduce the first release." Once people know where you stand, you can add the reasoning. The guide to not rambling in meetings covers what to do after that first sentence.

Look up while you pause

Where you look during a pause changes how it comes across, and de Montebello's most concrete tactic is about exactly that.

Think up. The player below is queued to 16:25, the exact moment Tristan de Montebello explains it. Watch on YouTube.

"It's a bad habit that a lot of people have, that when I am trying to gather my thoughts or think, people tend to look down. And if you're looking down on Zoom, it's three times as bad, because it looks like you're looking at your phone or looking at notes if you had any. But even when you're in person, it doesn't look very confident. ... As long as you're looking up, you actually look thoughtful by default. ... That means you look more confident, because anybody who'd be willing to pause in their speaking is somebody who's confident. And as a result, you're much less likely to get interrupted."
Tristan de Montebello, co-founder of Ultraspeaking · Lenny's Podcast

He recommends writing "think up" on a Post-it and sticking it on your monitor until the habit takes over. It matters most on video calls, where a downward glance during a silent moment looks like you're checking your phone.

Build the pause around your breath

Pausing gets harder when you're speaking on the last of your air. Matt Abrahams points out that nerves make us breathe shallow, and shallow breath changes how you sound, because "our voice is a wind instrument" (26:26). His fix is a specific breathing pattern.

The rule of lung. The player below is queued to 26:52, the exact moment Matt Abrahams explains it. Watch on YouTube.

"The bottom line is this. What's critical to the relaxation response is the exhale. Lots of ways to inhale, lots of ways to exhale. You want your exhale to be longer than the inhale. The magic of the relaxation happens during the exhale. So I have a rule of thumb, I jokingly call it a rule of lung. You want your exhale to be twice as long as your inhale. So if you take a three count in, even if you sneak in a little extra air as we were talking about, take a six count out."
Matt Abrahams, lecturer at Stanford Graduate School of Business · Lenny's Podcast

Before a high-stakes call, take two or three of those breaths: three counts in, six counts out. During the conversation, breathe at the ends of sentences instead of grabbing air mid-phrase, where the intake sounds like an interruption. If nerves are the bigger problem, the guide to being more confident in meetings goes deeper on that side.

Put silence around the sentence that matters

Pick the one line in your update or presentation that you want people to repeat afterward. Slow down slightly before it, say it cleanly, and leave a beat after it.

Project update

  • "The launch is still on schedule." [pause] "The risk is approval, not engineering capacity."

Recommendation

  • "I recommend we stop the pilot." [pause] "Usage has declined for six straight weeks, and the support cost now exceeds the value."

The beat after the conclusion keeps your evidence from burying the headline. It also gives people a moment to write the claim down or question it. Without any pauses, every sentence sounds equally important, and listeners have to guess which one you cared about.

Ask a question, then actually wait

Abrahams has a reframe for presentations that builds pauses in from the start: run the talk as a conversation, even when you're the only one speaking.

Structure the talk as questions. The player below is queued to 22:01, the exact moment Matt Abrahams explains it. Watch on YouTube.

"Many of us feel much more comfortable in conversation than we do in presenting. And conversation is a back and forth. So you can actually set up a lot of your communication situations, a presentation, a meeting, etc., as conversations. And you don't even have to have a conversation with the audience. You can simply have it with yourself. For example, what would it be like if you were to start a presentation by saying, today I have three questions I'd like to answer. Question number one is, and you state the question, and then you answer it. ... I'm not presenting. I'm having a conversation. And in so doing, it ratchets down that anxiety."
Matt Abrahams, lecturer at Stanford Graduate School of Business · Lenny's Podcast

Each question you pose gives you a natural pause before the answer. And when you ask the group a real question, wait long enough for someone to respond. Five seconds of silence after a reflective question is reasonable. If nobody answers, make the question smaller: "What's the biggest delivery risk you see?" Then wait again.

Protect your pause in fast meetings

Some teams treat any silence as an open floor. You can keep your thinking space without sounding territorial.

Mark the size of your answer

  • "I have two parts to that." [pause] "First, the customer signal is strong..."

Recover after an interruption

  • "I want to finish the second point, then I'd like your reaction."

Ask for thinking time

  • "I want to give you a useful answer. Let me take a moment."

Don't speed up after an interruption. Go back to your normal pace, finish the thought, and hand the floor over clearly. Rushing tells the group the interruption worked, so you'll get more of them. If you tend to fill the reclaimed space with "um" and "so," the guide to cutting filler words covers where those come from.

Practice pausing on purpose

A pause feels much longer from inside your own head than it sounds to anyone else. Recording yourself is the fastest way to recalibrate. A five-minute exercise:

  1. Pick a real update you'll give this week and write three short points.
  2. Mark the boundaries. Add a slash where you'll stop and breathe.
  3. Circle one headline sentence and give it a full beat before and after.
  4. Record the update on your phone. Keep your face and shoulders settled during the silences.
  5. Listen without watching. Notice whether the pauses make the message clearer.
  6. Record it once more. Shorten any pause that breaks a thought and stretch one that felt rushed.

Ultraspeaking turns this kind of drill into a game. De Montebello practices with slides that force him to change intensity on command, including one that tells him to stop talking.

A slide that says breathe. The player below is queued to 36:00, the exact moment Tristan de Montebello describes it, right before he plays the game on camera. Watch on YouTube.

"And then there might be a slide that says, breathe, which is just an indication to pause. And when I see that slide, breathe, if I just go silent, that's because I'm in front of the breathe slide and I'm not allowed to speak. And in that moment, my goal is just to relax myself and calm myself, and then see what happens, where I'm at when that slide moves on to the next one."
Tristan de Montebello, co-founder of Ultraspeaking · Lenny's Podcast

You can copy the drill without the software: put a card that says "breathe" in the middle of your speaker notes, and when you reach it, stop talking for one breath before you continue. The point of the drill is proving to yourself that you can go quiet for a moment and nothing bad happens. That's the proof de Montebello says most speakers have never given themselves.

Frequently asked questions

Will pausing make me sound unprepared?

A clean pause after a complete phrase usually comes across as composure. What changes the impression is trailing off, apologizing, or looking panicked. Prepare your opening sentence, keep your posture settled, and look up while you think.

Where should I look while pausing?

In person, keep soft eye contact with the group or glance briefly at your notes. On video, keep your face toward the camera and look up rather than down, since looking down during silence looks like you're reading your phone. Don't dart your eyes around; that makes the pause look like panic.

Can I pause in a job interview?

Yes. A short pause before a hard question is normal, and interviewers usually see it as taking the question seriously. If you need more time, say "Let me think about the best example." The behavioral interview guide covers how to structure the answer once you start.

How do I stop filling pauses with "so" or "um"?

Finish the phrase with your lips closed, breathe silently, then start the next complete sentence. Work on one transition at a time, and expect it to feel slow before it feels normal. The filler words guide goes deeper on the pressure moments that create them.

The pause playbook

Before you speak

  • Take the beat. Choose your first sentence in silence, then say it.
  • Before a high-stakes call, breathe three counts in and six counts out, two or three times.

While you're talking

  • Finish the phrase, breathe at the boundary, then start the next point.
  • Give your headline sentence a quiet beat on both sides.
  • Look up while you're thinking instead of down at your notes.

When you ask a question

  • Wait about five seconds. If nobody answers, ask a smaller question and wait again.

When you get interrupted

  • Say how many points you have, reclaim the unfinished one calmly, and return to your normal pace.

In practice

  • Record a real update, listen without watching, and judge the pauses by how they sound instead of how they felt.
