Behavioral interview questions: how top operators actually answer them

Leaders at Stripe, Ramp, Intercom, and Meta have explained on the record what they ask candidates and what they listen for. Here is what they said, with links to the exact moments.

12 min read

You're prepping for interviews by guessing what the interviewer wants to hear. You don't have to guess. Hiring leaders at Stripe, Ramp, Webflow, Intercom, and Meta have said on the record which behavioral questions they ask and what they listen for in the answers.

This article covers those questions, what a good answer sounds like, and how to prepare stories that hold up. Work through it before your next interview loop starts, because most people only get good at this around their tenth interview, and you can get there before your first. Every quote plays the exact moment it was said, so you can hear the full context.

What behavioral questions are actually testing

Start with why these questions exist. Organizational psychologist Adam Grant dedicated an episode of WorkLife to what's broken in interviews. His guest Lauren Rivera, who studies hiring at Northwestern, put it bluntly:

WorkLife with Adam Grant: reinventing the job interview. This one is audio only. Rivera's quote is at 10:06 and the structured-interview discussion runs from 12:16; the player starts from the top, or open it on Apple Podcasts cued to 10:06.

"In the United States, we love open-ended job interviews where people ask you about your hobbies... Why do you want to work here? And we know from research, those are some of the worst predictors of job performance you can have."
Lauren Rivera, Northwestern Kellogg · WorkLife with Adam Grant, "Reinventing the Job Interview" (10:06, audio)

The fix, Grant explains in the same episode, is structure: companies define the skills the job needs, ask every candidate the same questions about them, and score the answers. He notes the evidence suggests this can double or triple interview accuracy. That is why serious companies lean on behavioral questions ("tell me about a time...") paired with situational ones ("what would you do if..."). Rivera adds the catch for candidates: obvious behavioral questions are easy to game, so good interviewers push into specifics.

The practical takeaway: every behavioral question scores you against a rubric you can mostly predict, built on agency, judgment, self-awareness, and how you operate with other people.

The questions top operators actually ask

Lenny Rachitsky asked over 100 product and engineering leaders for their favorite interview question, then compiled the best answers into one episode. It is the closest thing to seeing the other team's playbook.

"What's the hardest thing you've ever done?"

What hard means. The player below is queued to 4:09, the exact moment Geoff Charles says it. Watch on YouTube.

"I asked, what's the hardest thing you've ever done? And I asked that because working at Ramp is hard. I want to understand what hard means for them."
Geoff Charles, VP of Product at Ramp

Charles listens for three things: why it was hard for you, how you worked with others through it, and how much of the outcome you personally drove. Pick a story where your own decisions, not circumstances, carried the result.

"Tell me about the work you're most proud of"

Taste and judgment. The player below is queued to 10:12, the exact moment Katie Dill says it. Watch on YouTube.

"Tell me what work you're most proud of. And the reason I ask that is because, well, it helps me understand their taste and their judgment, what motivates them, what work they view as good and as a good outcome."
Katie Dill, Head of Design at Stripe

Three different guests in that episode independently named this same question. Your answer reveals your bar for quality. If your proudest work is "we shipped on time," that is your bar. Pick something where you can articulate why the craft mattered.

Questions about ambiguity

Structure through ambiguity. The player below is queued to 11:53, the exact moment Jiaona Zhang says it. Watch on YouTube.

"Good answers are people who put structure and a way forward through the ambiguity, like that's what you look for."
Jiaona Zhang, VP of Product at Webflow

When the question is about a messy situation, interviewers grade how you created structure: how you framed the problem, sequenced the work, and pulled in the right inputs before committing. They care less than they say about whether the mess actually got fixed.

"What conventional wisdom do you think is hogwash?"

Breaking the interview script. The player below is queued to 16:53, the exact moment Nikhyl Singhal says it. Watch on YouTube.

"I'm always looking for people to break this sort of interview mindset. So everyone always prepares for interviews and then the entire conversation is predicting what you think you want me to say."
Nikhyl Singhal, VP of Product at Meta

Some interviewers deliberately break your script. You cannot prep an answer to this one, but you can prep the habit it is testing: having real opinions from your own experience and being willing to say them.

The question they ask your references

The reference-call question. The player below is queued to 20:09, the exact moment Paul Adams says it. Watch on YouTube.

"So this question is, what feedback will I be giving this person in their first performance review? And it's an amazing question because the person can't dodge it, you know?"
Paul Adams, Chief Product Officer at Intercom

Adams asks this on reference calls. Matt Mochary, who coaches CEOs at companies like Coinbase and OpenAI, teaches hiring managers the same thing in his curriculum: the predictive information does not come from the interviews at all. Strong processes use your interview mainly to collect names, then verify your stories through references. Which means your behavioral answers need to be true, specific, and consistent with what your former colleagues would say.

How to prepare: three stories and a narrative

On My First Million, Shaan Puri broke down his prep method for behavioral rounds: walk in with three stories already rehearsed.

My First Million: 5 hacks to landing your dream job. This one is audio only. The three-stories advice is at 7:12, the tell-me-about-yourself formula at 4:31, and the do-the-work story at 10:20; the player starts from the top, or open it on Apple Podcasts cued to 4:31.

"They're asking you for a story. And these behavioral interview questions are very common where people are going to ask you about, you know, tell me about a time when this happened. So you want to come prepared with three times."
Shaan Puri · My First Million, "5 Hacks to Landing Your Dream Job" (7:12, audio)

His three: a time you overcame an unexpected obstacle, a time you dealt with a hard people situation, and a time you spotted an opportunity nobody else saw. Most behavioral questions are a version of one of these three.

For "tell me about yourself," Puri uses a pivot-point structure (4:31 in the same episode): I grew up thinking X, then something punched me in the face and I realized X was wrong, here is what I did about it, and that is why I am sitting here. It works because it is an actual story instead of a walk through your resume.

And his favorite differentiator, from a candidate who interviewed 10 of the company's customers before the interview (10:20):

"Wow, this person did the work. They showed me they could do the job by doing the job beforehand. That is the easiest way to paint a picture in my head that you can do it is by having already done it."
Shaan Puri · My First Million, "5 Hacks to Landing Your Dream Job" (10:20)

Practice telling your story before interview ten

Lauren Ipsen recruits executives for Daversa Partners, one of tech's top search firms. She sees hundreds of candidates work through the same behavioral questions, and she names the two most common failures.

The first failure: not practicing.

Practice telling your story. The player below is queued to 54:19, the exact moment. Watch on YouTube.

"Being able to tell your story, like practice telling your story. So often people don't do that, especially when they're not looking for jobs. And then it's not until their 10th interview that they know how to tell their story."
Lauren Ipsen, Daversa Partners

The second is blame. Asked about missed deadlines or failed launches, candidates reflexively point at the engineering team, the exec team, the market (53:45). She flags this as an instant negative signal. The fix: own your part. Say what you saw late, what you would do differently, what you changed afterward. Nobody expects the story to have gone well; they expect you to know your role in it.

The gold-medal move: write the job description yourself

Phyl Terry, author of Never Search Alone, gave Lenny's audience a tactic that turns behavioral prep inside out. Before interviewing, privately write your own version of the job description: a job mission with OKRs for your first year.

The job mission with OKRs. The player below is queued to 50:24, the exact moment he explains the exercise. Watch on YouTube.

"When you start interviewing I want you to create your own version of the job description. I want you to do it privately, Lenny, and I want you to create what I call a job mission with OKRs."
Phyl Terry, author of Never Search Alone

This mirrors how the best hiring managers already operate. Mochary's curriculum tells managers to write a new hire's first-90-days goals before they even start recruiting. When you show up talking about the outcomes you would own in your first two quarters, you are answering the question they wrote down before they met you.

Terry also gives candidates a behavioral question to ask the interviewer (1:42:57 in the same episode): "Tell me about a time the company brought in a senior person and it failed." Senior hires fail often, the answer tells you why they fail here, and asking it signals you think about roles the way an owner does.

One more filter: expect the anti-sell

One pattern from the hiring side worth knowing before your next onsite. Mochary teaches companies to run an anti-sell: surface the hardest, ugliest parts of the job in the first conversation and see who stays interested. If an interviewer starts listing reasons the job is brutal, that is often deliberate. The winning response is a yes that shows you know the downsides and want the job anyway. Nikhyl Singhal's Skip Podcast episode on mastering your 30-second resume is a good companion here: it covers compressing your story to survive exactly these filters.

Prep checklist

Write

  • Three stories: unexpected obstacle, hard people situation, opportunity you spotted. Rehearse them out loud.
  • "Tell me about yourself" as a pivot-point narrative, under 90 seconds.
  • Your proudest work, and why it was good, not just that it shipped.

Pressure-test

  • For each story, name what you personally drove and what you got wrong. Zero blame on counterparts.
  • Check every story against what your references would say. Assume they will be asked.

Go further

  • Draft a job mission with first-year OKRs for the role, and bring that thinking into the conversation.
