How to answer "tell me about yourself" without reciting your resume

A 60 to 90 second opening that tells the interviewer what you do now, proves it with a result or two, and explains why this role comes next.

11 min read

You've probably answered it the way most people do: start at your first job, walk forward through every title, and hope the interviewer picks out what matters. Or you've gone the other way and opened with a polished one-liner that gives them nothing to ask about.

This article covers what the interviewer actually needs from the question, a three-part structure for the answer, five worked examples for different career situations, and a 15-minute rehearsal plan. Build the answer once, then adjust the ending for each role you interview for.

The advice comes from Phyl Terry, whose book Never Search Alone has put thousands of candidates through job search councils; Lauren Ipsen, who recruits senior executives at Daversa Partners; and Matt Abrahams, who lectures on strategic communication at Stanford Graduate School of Business.

The three-part answer

Present: "I lead customer operations for a B2B software company, with a focus on making complex implementations predictable."

Proof: "Over the last three years, I redesigned our enterprise handoff and cut median launch time by 18 days while our customer base doubled."

Path: "I'm looking for a role where I can apply that operating experience across a larger portfolio, which is what drew me to this position."

Know what the interviewer needs from the question

"Tell me about yourself" usually comes first, and your answer shapes where the interviewer digs for the rest of the conversation. They're listening for a few specific things: how you describe your professional identity, evidence that you can do this job, whether your transitions make sense, how you organize an answer when nobody has given you a structure, and why you want this move.

A good answer also sets up two or three follow-up paths you'd be happy to take: the big result you delivered, the transition that widened your scope, or the reason this company's problem fits what you want next. You're choosing the questions you'll get for the next twenty minutes, so only mention things you'd be glad to get a question about.

Work backward from the job's mission

Most job postings list responsibilities without saying what the company actually needs to change. Phyl Terry's fix: before you interview, write your own private version of the job description, framed as a mission with measurable outcomes. It forces you to decide what the company is really hiring this person to accomplish.

The private job mission. The player below is queued to 50:24, the exact moment Phyl Terry says it. Watch on YouTube.

"When you start interviewing, I want you to create your own version of the job description. I want you to do it privately... and I want you to create what I call a job mission with OKRs... something where you are saying, here's what I think I'm going to be accountable for, here's the outcomes I'm going to deliver at the company you join."
Phyl Terry, author of Never Search Alone · Lenny's Podcast

Write down three outcomes you think the role owns. Then pick the parts of your background that best predict those outcomes. Once you know what the role is supposed to accomplish, your introduction can point straight at it, and the same document gives you better questions to ask them.

Build the answer as present, proof, and path

  1. Present: name your current professional focus in one or two sentences. What you do and the kind of problem you handle well.
  2. Proof: name the theme that runs through your work and back it with one or two concrete results.
  3. Path: connect that evidence to the problem, scope, or environment in this role.

Keep the timeline selective. Mention an earlier role only when it explains the theme or a transition. Save the rich detail for follow-up questions, where it counts for more because the interviewer asked for it.

Practice the story before you need it

Lauren Ipsen has watched candidates reach a tenth interview before they can explain clearly what they did, what changed because of their work, and why the organization missed them after they left. By then they'd already burned interviews at companies they actually wanted while they figured out how to talk about themselves.

The story most people learn too late. The player below is queued to 54:19, the exact moment Lauren Ipsen says it. Watch on YouTube.

"Being able to tell your story, like practice telling your story. So often people don't do that, especially when they're not looking for jobs. And then it's not until their tenth interview that they know how to tell their story and what they did and what they impacted and why they were someone to be missed when they moved on."
Lauren Ipsen, executive recruiter at Daversa Partners · Lenny's Podcast

Write bullets first instead of full sentences. Record one attempt, listen once, and fix the spots where the relevance isn't clear. Don't polish every word. You want the facts and the order to stay stable while the sentences change a little each time, because a memorized script falls apart the moment the interviewer phrases the question differently.

Five example answers

Experienced individual contributor

"I'm a data scientist focused on pricing and demand forecasting. In my current role, I built a forecasting system used by six regional teams and reduced planning error by 22 percent. The common thread in my work is turning ambiguous commercial questions into models operators can actually use. I'm interested in this role because it combines deeper modeling with direct ownership of pricing decisions."

Career changer

"I've spent seven years in customer success, most recently leading enterprise renewals. While doing that work, I kept gravitating toward the systems behind customer health, so I learned SQL and built an early-risk model that our team adopted. I'm now moving into analytics because that's where I've been creating the most leverage. This role stands out because the first priority is product adoption, a problem I already understand from the customer side."

Recent graduate

"I recently completed a computer science degree with a concentration in human-computer interaction. My most relevant work was a six-month accessibility project with the campus library, where I led user testing and shipped improvements used across its booking tools. I'm looking for an early-career role where I can combine front-end engineering with direct user research, which is why this product team caught my attention."

Senior leader

"I lead operations for a 400-person services organization. Over the last four years, I've focused on scaling delivery without losing customer trust, including a regional redesign that improved gross margin by seven points and raised retention. The next challenge I want is building that operating system across an international business. Your expansion plan and the need to integrate three acquired teams make this role especially relevant."

After a layoff

"I was most recently a product marketing manager at Meridian, where I led positioning and launch strategy for its mid-market platform. My role was eliminated when the company consolidated two product lines in May. The work I want to continue is bringing technical products into clear customer language; our last launch increased qualified pipeline by 31 percent. I'm interested in this role because the company is entering a similarly complex market."

Give the follow-up answers a clean shape

A good opening earns follow-ups: "Tell me more about that launch" or "How much of the strategy did you own?" Matt Abrahams teaches a structure for these spontaneous answers that he calls ADD: answer the question, give a detailed example, then describe why it matters for this role.

Answer, example, relevance. The player below is queued to 1:04:20, the exact moment Matt Abrahams explains it. Watch on YouTube.

"It's called ADD, for adding value. You answer the question cleanly and concisely, you then give an example to reinforce the answer, and then you explain the relevance or significance of the answer so people know it."
Matt Abrahams, lecturer at Stanford Graduate School of Business · Lenny's Podcast

ADD works for direct questions about your experience or opinions. Behavioral follow-ups ("tell me about a conflict", "a time a plan fell apart") need prepared stories instead. The behavioral interview questions playbook has the full question bank and what hiring leaders at Stripe, Ramp, Webflow, and Meta say they listen for in the answers.

Fix the five common problems

  • The life story: start at your current professional focus and include personal history only when it explains a career choice.
  • The resume recital: pick a theme and evidence instead of listing every role.
  • The vague claim: replace "I'm passionate about impact" with the problem you solve and a result.
  • The unexplained transition: give the reason for a move in one calm sentence, then keep going.
  • The missing destination: end with why this role follows from the story you just told.

Rehearse in 15 minutes

  1. Minutes 1 to 4: write one bullet each for present, proof, and path.
  2. Minutes 5 to 7: say the answer out loud without reading full sentences.
  3. Minutes 8 to 10: cut any detail that doesn't help the interviewer evaluate you for this role.
  4. Minutes 11 to 13: record one more attempt and check that it runs 60 to 90 seconds.
  5. Minutes 14 to 15: practice the most likely first follow-up with ADD.

Frequently asked questions

How long should my answer be?

Aim for 60 to 90 seconds. A senior or complicated background might need closer to two minutes, but start with the shorter version and let the interviewer ask for detail.

Should I mention hobbies or family?

Include personal information when it adds relevant context or the interviewer explicitly invites it. Keep the professional thread clear either way.

How do I explain a job gap?

Name the gap briefly and factually, mention relevant work or learning if there was any, and return to the evidence that fits this role. See the layoff example above for the shape.

Can I use the same answer for every company?

Keep the present and the core evidence stable. Change the path, and sometimes the proof, so the answer points at the outcomes of each specific role.

Should I memorize my answer?

Memorize the structure and the facts, then keep the wording flexible. You'll sound natural and you can still respond to the question the interviewer actually asked.

The answer on one page

Before you write

  • Draft the job's mission privately: three outcomes you think the role owns.
  • Pick the parts of your background that best predict those outcomes.

The answer

  • Present: what you do and the problem you handle well.
  • Proof: the pattern in your work and one or two measurable results.
  • Path: why this role is a credible next step.
  • Length: 60 to 90 seconds.

Practice

  • Bullets first, then two recordings, listened back to once each.
  • One likely follow-up answered with ADD: answer, detailed example, relevance.
