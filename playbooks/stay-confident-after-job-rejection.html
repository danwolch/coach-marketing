How to stay confident when your job search is full of rejection

Most of what a job search sends back is silence and no. Here's how to read those rejections accurately and keep showing up strong.

11 min read

Two things make rejection hurt more than it has to: treating every no as a verdict on your ability, and going through the whole search without telling anyone how it's really going.

This article covers why rejection hits so hard, what a rejection can and can't tell you, and the habits that keep your confidence intact for the interviews that matter. Set up the support structure and the weekly routine first, because you'll need both before the next no arrives.

The advice comes from Phyl Terry, whose book Never Search Alone has organized thousands of job seekers into councils; Lauren Ipsen, who recruits senior executives at Daversa Partners; Deb Liu, CEO of Ancestry and former VP of Product at Facebook; Nikhyl Singhal, who ran product at Meta and Credit Karma; and organizational psychologist Adam Grant. Every quote plays the exact moment it was said.

Why rejection hits this hard

Rejection hurts more than it seems like it should, and there's a physical reason. On his WorkLife podcast, Adam Grant describes brain-scan studies where people were quietly excluded from a simple ball-tossing game. When the other players stopped passing to them, their brains lit up in patterns similar to processing physical pain. Getting excluded registers in the body, and a rejection email is a small version of exactly that.

A job search makes that worse in two specific ways. First, the volume: you might absorb more rejection in three months of searching than in ten years of employment. Second, the timing: it arrives exactly when you've lost the everyday evidence that you're good at your work. Nobody is asking for your opinion, you're not shipping anything, and no performance review is telling you what you did well. So the rejections arrive while very little else is arriving to balance them out.

So if you feel worse than you think you should, you're not being weak about it. You're having a normal reaction to a genuinely hard situation. The goal of everything below is to keep that reaction from making your decisions for you.

One scope note: this page is about rejection from other companies. If the rejection came from inside, a promotion you didn't get, the politics and the next moves are different. The promotion denied playbook covers that conversation.

What a rejection actually tells you

A rejection feels like a judgment of you. Most of the time it's a judgment of a match, made under constraints you never saw: an internal candidate, a budget that changed, a hiring manager who wanted a background slightly different from yours, a stronger candidate who applied the same week. A recruiter compresses all of that into a two-line email that explains none of it.

Grant's episode on rejection makes this concrete. Psychologists used to study two ways of explaining a setback, blaming yourself and blaming others, and both have costs, since one wrecks your confidence and the other stops you learning anything. The research points at a third explanation that's usually more accurate.

WorkLife with Adam Grant: Bouncing Back from Rejection. This one is audio only. The quote is at 12:39; the player starts from the top, or open it on Apple Podcasts cued to 12:39.

"Recently, researchers have discovered that there's a third option. Most of the time when you get rejected, the main reason is not you. It's not me. It's us."
Adam Grant, organizational psychologist at Wharton · WorkLife with Adam Grant (12:39, audio)

In the same episode, executive Sarah Robb O'Hagan looks back on being fired from Atari and concludes the rejection was a mismatch she could have predicted: she was marketing video games and she didn't like video games. She could have concluded she was bad at marketing, which would have hurt and also been wrong. Calling it the wrong company for her was both accurate and something she could act on.

Phyl Terry, who has watched thousands of searches through his job search councils, says the same thing about today's market: "There's not something wrong with you. This is the market that we're in." A search that took six weeks in 2021 can take six months now, at the same level of talent.

What one rejection can tell you

  • This particular match didn't work, for reasons that may have nothing to do with you.
  • If they named a gap, one data point about how you came across that day.
  • Which stage you reached, which tells you what's already working.

What one rejection cannot tell you

  • Whether you're good at your job.
  • Whether the next company will decide the same way.
  • Anything at all, until you compare it with your other interviews and look for a repeat.

The fastest way to stop reading a rejection as a verdict is to price it in before it happens. Austin Belcak, who coaches job seekers at Cultivated Culture, treats the search as a funnel where each stage converts at roughly 30 percent, his observed average across clients. Work backward from two offers and the volume of no is already on the page before you send a single application.

Belcak's funnel math

  • 2 offers ← 6 final rounds
  • 6 final rounds ← roughly 18 phone screens
  • 18 phone screens ← roughly 54 real conversations
  • 54 conversations ← roughly 162 people contacted

Written that way, the search is 160 people telling you no in various ways, and the two offers still arrive. A rejection at any stage is the 70 percent doing what the 70 percent does. Track your own rates per stage and the number stops being emotional at all, because it becomes the step where your funnel needs work.

The Dream Job System: the funnel technique. This one is audio only. The quote is at 5:26; the player starts from the top, or open it on Apple Podcasts cued to 5:26.

"Hey, this funnel starts with 162 networking emails. That's where I'm going to start. Or this funnel starts with 162 online applications, whatever that is for you. But then you're going to start optimizing."
Austin Belcak, Cultivated Culture · The Dream Job System

Debrief every interview, win or lose

The fastest way to stop rejections from turning into a story about your worth is to turn each one into notes. The same day as the interview, before any decision comes back, write down every question you remember, the answer you gave, and the moments the interviewer leaned in or checked out. You'll forget the details within about a day, but you'll still remember how it felt, and how it felt is usually a bad guide to how it went.

When a rejection does arrive, add it to the record instead of replaying it. Note the stage you reached and, if you can get it, one piece of feedback. Then apply a simple threshold: one rejection is noise. The same gap named by two different interviewers, or three processes that all end at the same stage, is signal, and signal is good news, because you can work on something specific instead of vaguely trying to be better.

Never search alone

Terry's central advice is structural. His book is built on job search councils: groups of six to eight job seekers who meet every two weeks, help each other figure out what the market wants from them, and keep each other going. He doesn't pitch the group as company for the lonely. He says something specific happens when anxious people stop hiding their search.

The anxiety flip. The player below is queued to 4:02, the exact moment Phyl Terry explains it. Watch on YouTube.

"There's this great hack. I didn't invent this, it's baked into human psychology. If you put anxious people together and ask them to be open and vulnerable and to ask for help... it actually flips the anxiety and the fear into hope, into motivation, into accountability and confidence."
Phyl Terry, author of Never Search Alone · Lenny's Podcast

The Never Search Alone community runs a free matching program that places you in a council. If a formal council feels like more than you want, build the minimum version: two other people who are also searching, a 30-minute call every week, and honest numbers. You'll discover that the people you respect are also getting ghosted, which does more for your confidence than any pep talk.

Terry adds one habit for the wider circle: send a short update to your network every month, even when there's no news. "It might be, I don't have a job yet, or I don't even have any news," he says, but the note keeps relationships warm and keeps you from disappearing into the search. He's seen long-term seekers stall for exactly this reason: they stopped networking, left their councils, and went quiet.

Keep your interview skills warm

Part of what feels like fading confidence is actually rust. Interviewing is a performance skill, and most people walk into their first interview in years having rehearsed nothing. Lauren Ipsen has recruited senior executives for two decades, and she watches strong candidates burn their best opportunities while they relearn how to talk about themselves.

The tenth-interview effect. The player below is queued to 54:18, the exact moment Lauren Ipsen describes it. Watch on YouTube.

"Being able to tell your story, like practice telling your story. So often people don't do that, especially when they're not looking for jobs. And then it's not until their tenth interview that they know how to tell their story and what they did and what they impacted and why they were someone to be missed when they moved on."
Lauren Ipsen, executive recruiter at Daversa Partners · Lenny's Podcast

You can compress those ten interviews into practice reps that cost you nothing. Say your story out loud until it's fluent: what you did, what it changed, why you'd be missed. Run mock interviews with a friend, a council member, or a coach. And sequence your pipeline deliberately, with companies you're curious about early and the roles you want most after you've had a few at-bats. The reps you do in practice are ones you don't have to burn on a company you actually want.

This also changes what a rejection means mid-search. If interviews three and four went badly, some of that is rust you're still shaking off. Judge yourself against how you interviewed in week one, not against how you'd like to be doing by now.

Routines that protect your confidence

Terry argues that job seekers manage the wrong asset. They polish the resume and the LinkedIn profile and never manage the thing that has more effect on how the search goes.

The emotional balance sheet. The player below is queued to 13:51, the exact moment Phyl Terry says it. Watch on YouTube.

"Most people think, like, what's the most important thing to manage during your job search? They think, well, maybe it's their resume or LinkedIn profile or their ability to network... But the most important thing to manage is your emotional balance. I talk about your emotional balance sheet. And for many job seekers, they have more liabilities than assets... They have more fear and anxiety. They feel demoralized. They have a hard time going."
Phyl Terry, author of Never Search Alone · Lenny's Podcast

Managing that balance sheet is mostly routine. Treat the search as a part-time job with set hours, roughly 20 to 25 a week, and close the laptop when the hours are done, because a search that runs all day produces the same output with triple the anxiety. Measure your week by what you controlled, applications sent and conversations held, rather than by offers. Keep one activity in your week that you're plainly good at, whether that's mentoring, a side project, or a sport, so the search isn't the only place anyone sees you be good at something. And protect sleep and exercise the way you would before a big presentation, because interviews are performances and you're doing them weekly.

If you're searching full time, Belcak prescribes an explicit daily allocation: four to six hours of real search work, two hours of self-care, and two hours on a hobby. The hobby is load-bearing, not decoration. Work provided identity and progress, and the search doesn't, so something else has to. His point about the marginal hour is worth holding onto: a seventh hour of applications won't make an offer arrive sooner, but it will make you worse in the interviews that matter.

Nikhyl Singhal spent an episode of The Skip coaching a design leader whose confidence had cratered after a few months of searching. His advice starts with pacing: treat the gap like an athlete's off-season, with deliberate recovery and skill work, and set your expectations for a long season. He also names the trap most people fall into in week one.

The spiral of interviewing too soon. The player below is queued to 33:45, the exact moment Nikhyl Singhal plays out what happens when you skip the pause. Watch on YouTube.

"Here's the concern: just came off this job, I abruptly had to leave, I'm immediately worried, so I immediately started interviewing. The interviews themselves didn't go great, partly because I was not ready, partly because I wasn't rested, partly because I was looking backwards. Now I've talked to four or five of the employers that are hiring, I'm not getting anywhere, I have increased anxiety... These are the things that we avoid by saying, pick a pause."
Nikhyl Singhal, host of The Skip, former Meta VP of Product · The Skip

The employers will still be hiring in six weeks. A rested candidate with a clear story beats an anxious one who started sooner, and the early interviews you rush into are usually with the companies you cared about most.

When to change strategy, and when to keep going

Confidence through rejection doesn't mean ignoring the data. Your interview log tells you which of three situations you're in, and each one calls for something different.

If applications go out and nothing comes back, your targeting or positioning is off. Terry's term for this is candidate-market fit: what the market will hire you for right now, which can sit below what you've done before. He tells stories of executives who found the market would only buy them one level down, took the role, and moved back up within a couple of years. Testing your positioning with real conversations beats six more months of identical applications.

If you're getting screens but no late rounds, or final rounds but no offers, the pipeline believes in you and some part of the story needs work. That's a practice problem, and the debrief log usually shows you exactly which answer to fix.

And if the process is working, interviews happening, stages advancing, feedback decent, then nothing is broken and the only strategy is persistence. Singhal is blunt about this part of the market: "If you don't have grit and hustle, you're not going to find a job in this market." A slow search is normal right now, and as long as it's still moving forward, it's working.

Deb Liu ran product at Facebook and PayPal before becoming CEO of Ancestry, and she's spent years watching which people come through setbacks stronger. Her observation applies to the search as much as to the job.

Stumbling blocks into stepping stones. The player below is queued to 11:41, the exact moment Deb Liu says it. Watch on YouTube.

"The people who are most successful are not the people that had no failures, who lived charmed lives and had up-and-to-the-right careers and got promoted every cycle. The people who were most successful were the ones who actually, through adversity, learned to turn stumbling blocks into stepping stones. They were the ones who got hard feedback and then came back stronger because now they learned what to do differently."
Deb Liu, CEO of Ancestry and former VP of Product at Facebook · Lenny's Podcast

Later in the same conversation she quotes a line she's carried for years, that life is 10% what happens to you and 90% how you react to it, and describes the person who says "I didn't get the job that I wanted and I'm just going to figure out another path" as the one who ends up with the most satisfying career. You don't get to control whether a company says no, but you do control what you do with it afterward and whether you keep the search moving.

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal to feel depressed during a job search?

Feeling low, anxious, and demoralized during a long search is extremely common, and the research Grant cites suggests rejection registers in the brain much like physical pain, so you're not overreacting. Structure helps: support people, set hours, and activities that remind you what you're good at. But if the low mood sticks around for weeks, or it's affecting your sleep, appetite, or ability to function, talk to a doctor or therapist. A job search problem and a depression problem can happen at the same time, and the second one deserves real treatment, not just better search tactics.

How do I stop taking job rejection personally?

Change what you do in the hour after the news. Write down the stage you reached, what you'd repeat, and one thing to test next time, then tell one person in your support circle. The mismatch framing helps too: most rejections mean the fit wasn't there, which says little about your ability. You'll believe that framing faster when your own log shows different companies valuing completely different things about you.

How many rejections are normal before an offer?

More than feels reasonable. In this market, strong candidates routinely see dozens of rejections and months of searching, and Grant's episode features a comedy writer, Emily Winter, who set herself a goal of 100 rejections in a year precisely because volume was the only path to the yeses. If your rejections mostly come after interviews, you're closer than the feeling suggests, because most applications die without a single conversation.

Should I take a break from my job search?

It's much better to plan a pause than to burn out into one. Singhal recommends taking real recovery time before you begin interviewing, then running the search with consistent effort. A planned week off mid-search can reset you, while drifting for months without deciding to makes it hard to restart. The distinction that matters is whether you chose the break and put an end date on it.

How do I walk into the next interview confident after a string of rejections?

Preparation you can point to will do more for you than trying to talk yourself into feeling good. Debrief the last interview so the lesson is captured, practice your opening story out loud, and remind yourself which stages you've been reaching, because that's evidence the market takes you seriously. The techniques in the confidence in meetings playbook, from preparation structure to managing your physical state, carry straight over to interviews.

The confidence routine on one page

Set up once

  • Join or build a support circle: a job search council or two fellow seekers on a weekly call.
  • Set search hours, roughly 20 to 25 a week, and a hard stop each day.
  • Pick one weekly activity where you're demonstrably good at something.

After every interview

  • Write down the questions, your answers, and the reactions, same day.
  • Debrief with one person before you decide how it went.

After every rejection

  • Log the stage reached and any feedback, then ask one narrow feedback question.
  • Name one thing to repeat and one thing to test next time.
  • Tell your support circle instead of carrying it alone.

Every month

  • Send a short update to your network, even with no news.
  • Review the log for patterns: same gap twice or processes ending at the same stage.
  • Decide deliberately: keep going as is, fix a specific answer, or retest your positioning.
