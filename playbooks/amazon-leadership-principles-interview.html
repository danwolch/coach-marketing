How to prepare for an Amazon Leadership Principles interview

Every interviewer is assigned specific principles to test you on. Here is how they score, and how to build stories that hold up when they start drilling.

14 min read

Are you preparing for Amazon by memorizing the Leadership Principles and hoping you can name one when a question sounds close? Then an interviewer asks about a time you had to make a decision without enough information, you tell a story you're proud of, and they spend the next twelve minutes asking what the numbers were, who disagreed with you, and what you'd do differently. Those twelve minutes are where most of your score gets decided, and almost nobody prepares for them.

This article covers what the Leadership Principles are for inside Amazon, and how interviewers score your answers against them. It also covers how to build a story bank for the principles you'll be asked about, and the follow-up questions almost nobody rehearses. Read it once, then spend the rest of your prep writing and saying stories out loud, because the failure mode here is having good experience and no organized way to talk about it.

The advice comes from Ethan Evans, who spent 15 years at Amazon rising to vice president, did more than a thousand interviews there, and hired roughly a thousand people; Dave Anderson, who went from junior manager to tech director at Amazon over 12 years and served as a bar raiser; and Bill Carr, who spent 15 years at Amazon, launched Amazon Music and Prime Video, was also a bar raiser, and co-wrote Working Backwards. Every quote plays the exact moment it was said.

One scoping note before we start. Loop structures, round counts, and the number of principles all change over time, and Amazon has revised the list more than once. This article teaches the evaluation model, which has been stable for close to two decades, rather than a current org chart. Read the principles on Amazon's own careers site before your loop, since a blog's copy of the list may be a version or two old.

What the Leadership Principles are actually for

Plenty of companies publish a list of values that nobody uses after onboarding. Amazon built processes around theirs. Evans, who joined in 2005 and left as a VP in 2020, describes three places the principles get used: hiring, annual reviews, and promotion documents. That's why they carry real weight in your interview instead of being a warm-up topic.

Each interviewer gets assigned two principles. The player below is queued to 9:43, the exact moment Ethan Evans explains how the interview mechanism works. Watch on YouTube.

"So when four or five people are chosen to interview someone, each person is usually assigned two leadership principles. So eight to ten of the 14 get covered. And the idea is you will interview the person and try to figure out, do they meet these principles? ... Is there evidence that in the past they have acted in these ways?"
Ethan Evans, retired Amazon vice president · Level Up

Two things follow from that. First, the questions that feel random are not random. The interviewer has an assignment, and they need evidence on those specific principles before the hour is up. Second, coverage matters more than you'd guess. If your best stories all demonstrate the same two or three principles, several interviewers will walk out of your loop with thin notes, and thin notes give the debrief very little to work with.

Anderson, who worked at Amazon for 12 years and wrote one of the most-read public explanations of the principles, says the reason they hold up in interviews is that they describe behavior rather than skill. He points at ownership as the one that pulls the others along.

Why ownership shows up in so many questions. The player below is queued to 20:52, the exact moment Dave Anderson explains what he means by acting like an owner. Watch on YouTube.

"I think it's an overriding thing of, if you're an owner, you will act in many of these ways. ... I'm not going to say I'm doing this because someone asked me to. I'm not doing it because I'm going to get a good rating. I'm not doing it because if I don't, I'm going to get in trouble. It's like, I want to do the right thing."
Dave Anderson, former Amazon tech director and bar raiser · Level Up

That's the test hiding inside a lot of these questions. Did you do the thing because it was assigned to you, or because you decided it was yours? An interviewer can hear the difference in about two sentences.

How much of the loop this decides

More than candidates expect. Evans is direct about the trade Amazon makes between hard skills and behavior, and he was on the hiring side of it for 15 years.

Fit gets more weight than expertise. The player below is queued to 5:34, the exact moment Ethan Evans says it. Watch on YouTube.

"What we have found over time is that your performance is more driven by your culture fit and native skills than by your specific expertise. So we check that you have domain expertise, but we focus on fit."
Ethan Evans, retired Amazon vice president · Level Up

Domain questions still happen, and in a technical loop they take real time. But a candidate who codes well and gives vague, credit-sharing answers about their past work tends to lose to a candidate who codes fine and can show exactly what they decided and why. If you're allocating prep hours, don't give the behavioral part the leftovers.

Worth knowing where the principles rank against each other, too. Evans points out the list runs from customer obsession at the top to delivering results at the bottom, and that Amazonians joke about which one really counts.

The internal joke about which principle wins. The player below is queued to 25:47, the exact moment Ethan Evans explains the order of the list and what people say about it. Watch on YouTube.

"There was a joke in Amazon that said not all the leadership principles are equal, it all comes down to how much results you deliver. And in a way, I agree with that. The rest of it is how you do it."
Ethan Evans, retired Amazon vice president · Level Up

Translate that into story selection. A story where you handled a process beautifully and shipped nothing is a weak story, whatever principle it was meant to demonstrate. Pick stories that ended in something happening.

Building a story bank

What you're preparing is a set of stories rather than a set of answers. Evans puts it plainly when someone asks how to signal that you match a company's principles without sounding like you're name-dropping them.

The interview is mostly storytelling. The player below is queued to 41:00, the exact moment Ethan Evans answers a viewer asking how to bring up a company's principles without forcing it. Watch on YouTube.

"You let someone know by telling stories. Interviews are usually mostly storytelling and fact sharing, and you share a story that seems to indicate that you do the thing in question. And you can name the principle or you can just talk about it."
Ethan Evans, retired Amazon vice president · Level Up

How many stories, and how to map them

Aim for eight to twelve stories from the last three to five years, written down. That sounds like a lot until you realize a good story covers two or three principles at once. A project you pushed through against internal resistance can serve ownership, backbone, and bias for action depending on which part you emphasize.

Then build the grid. List the current principles down one side and your stories across the top, and mark which stories can credibly serve which principle. The gaps are the point of the exercise. If nothing in your bank shows frugality, or dive deep, or earning trust after you got something wrong, you have a hole that an assigned interviewer will land on. Go find an example from further back, or accept that you'll answer that one thinly and put your energy elsewhere.

What belongs in each story

Amazon interviewers are trained on behavioral interviewing and take notes in a STAR-shaped structure: situation, task, action, result. The version candidates give is usually too short at both ends. They spend three minutes on background and then say "and it worked out well."

Write each story with these parts

  • Situation, in two or three sentences. Enough for someone outside your industry to follow. Include when it happened and how big the thing was.
  • Your task, stated as a decision you owned. "The team needed to reduce churn" tells an interviewer nothing about you, so say what you specifically were responsible for.
  • Actions, in first person singular. What you chose, what you rejected, who you had to convince, what you did when the first approach failed.
  • Result with numbers. Percentage, dollars, time saved, defect rate, headcount, dates. If you genuinely can't share a figure, give a proportion or a before-and-after comparison.
  • What you'd do differently. One real thing, with what you learned from it. This gets asked constantly and unprepared answers sound either defensive or fake.

The numbers matter more here than in most interview formats, because "be right a lot" and "dive deep" are principles someone will be assigned to test, and both get tested by asking how you knew. The follow-up questions nobody rehearses

Your opening answer is mostly raw material. The interviewer takes it and starts digging, and what they find is what they write down. Bill Carr, who spent 15 years at Amazon and interviewed as a bar raiser, describes spending an entire hour on a single example.

One question, one hour. The player below is queued to 1:28:50, the exact moment Bill Carr gives his favorite interview question and what he does with the answer. Watch on YouTube.

"Tell me about their most significant professional accomplishment. And I have to always clarify this, by this I mean not some award you won or some promotion you got, I mean something you built, some product, some process, some organization you built. ... Once they get into that example, ask a lot of probing and follow-up questions. I could fill an entire hour interview just sticking with this one example."
Bill Carr, former Amazon VP and co-author of Working Backwards · Lenny's Podcast

Carr says he's trying to understand everything from the situation to the result, including who the candidate influenced, what decisions they made, and what roadblocks they hit. So prepare for the drilling, not the opener. For each story in your bank, have answers ready for these:

Follow-ups to prepare for every story

  • What was the actual number? Before and after.
  • How did you know that was the right call? What data did you have, and what did you have to assume?
  • Who disagreed with you, and what did they say?
  • What did you do when the first approach didn't work?
  • What was your specific contribution versus your team's?
  • What would you do differently now?
  • What did your manager think? What feedback did you get afterward?
  • How long did it take, and why that long?

Two of those deserve extra attention. "Who disagreed with you" is a disguised test of have backbone, disagree and commit, which both Carr and Anderson describe as the principle people understand worst. Anderson's version: you argue hard, you lose, and then you go make the decision succeed without ever saying "I told you so." If nobody ever pushed back on you, the interviewer concludes either that the project didn't matter much or that you avoid conflict.

And "what was your specific contribution" catches most people, because normal professional courtesy trains you to say we. Describe your team's work honestly, then be precise about which parts you decided, wrote, negotiated, or unblocked.

What the bar raiser does

Somewhere in your loop is an interviewer who doesn't work for the hiring manager and has no stake in filling the role. Carr, who was one, explains the design.

Who the bar raiser is and what power they have. The player below is queued to 1:16:26, the exact moment Bill Carr describes the bar raiser role in the loop and the debrief. Watch on YouTube.

"On every interview loop there's one person who is not the hiring manager, who doesn't report to the hiring manager, is not the recruiting manager... they are on the interview loop and they're a bar raiser, which means when we get to the debrief meeting they will run that meeting, not the hiring manager, not the recruiter. And it also means that they technically have veto power over the hiring manager, which by the way a good bar raiser never uses."
Bill Carr, former Amazon VP and co-author of Working Backwards · Lenny's Podcast

Carr says the role started in 1999 because Amazon was growing so fast that new hires were hiring new hires, and everyone was importing whatever standards they'd used at their last company. The bar raiser exists to counterweight a hiring manager's urgency to fill the seat, and to hold the debrief to the written criteria rather than gut feel.

What that means for you as a candidate is small but useful. You cannot tell which interviewer is the bar raiser, and guessing is a waste of attention. They're often not the most senior person on your schedule, and Carr notes it's a good role for people earlier in their careers. Assume every interviewer's notes get read out and challenged, and don't save your best material for whoever looks most important.

It also explains why consistency matters across the whole day. If you tell two interviewers versions of the same project with different numbers in them, the bar raiser will catch it in the debrief, because that's the job. Where candidates lose points

Evans and Anderson have each sat through hundreds of debriefs. The failure patterns repeat.

The ones that come up over and over

  • Saying we when you mean I. The interviewer is scoring one person. If they can't tell what you did, they have nothing to write down.
  • No numbers. "It was a big success" gives a note-taker nothing. Bring the metric, even an imperfect one, and say how you measured it.
  • Answers that sound recited. A story delivered word for word at the same pace regardless of the question sounds memorized, and the follow-ups expose it fast. Know the facts of your stories cold, and let the wording come out fresh each time.
  • Reusing one story for everything. Interviewers compare notes. Four people hearing about the same launch is a coverage problem for the loop and a range problem for you.
  • Naming the principle instead of demonstrating it. Evans says a skilled candidate can work the words in, but if it doesn't come out naturally, skip it. What the interviewer writes down is the story, not the label you put on it.
  • A weakness answer with no weakness in it. "What would you do differently" answered with "nothing, it went great" tells the interviewer you don't examine your own work.
  • Flat energy. Evans says appearance and enthusiasm rank shockingly high in interview outcomes, and that interviewers form an impression in the first minute and then collect evidence for it. That's hard to sustain across a full day of interviews, so plan for it.

If your instinct is to soften your own role because claiming credit feels arrogant, that's worth practicing away from before the loop. Our behavioral interview questions playbook has the general version of this problem, and the fix is the same: describe the work accurately, then say which parts were your calls.

If you've never worked at Amazon

Most candidates haven't, and the principles were written to describe how anyone competent works rather than anything Amazon-specific. Anderson, who has watched other companies adopt the list wholesale for their own interviews, argues they're a clear way to name behaviors any employer wants. Your experience translates fine. You just have to frame it so an Amazon interviewer can score it.

How to translate what you've done

  • Scale is relative and interviewers know it. A 12-person startup story is fine. Say the size out loud so your result gets read in context, then focus on the decision quality.
  • Frugality means resourcefulness. Anderson's version is that a team of seven that should have been fifteen often does something remarkable because it only had seven. Times you got more from less, or shut down something that wasn't working, both count.
  • Customer means whoever you serve. Internal teams, patients, students, and field technicians all work. Say who they are and what you learned from talking to them directly.
  • Dive deep means you know the details. Be ready to go two or three levels below your summary on any story: the actual query, the actual conversation, the actual line item.
  • Learn the vocabulary, then mostly leave it alone. Knowing what dive deep and bias for action mean helps you pick the right story. Sprinkling the phrases through every answer doesn't.

One more thing that explains why Amazon interviews feel the way they do. The company runs on written narratives instead of slide decks, and Carr describes the point of that as forcing people to expose their thinking deeply rather than gesturing at a concept. The interview is the same instinct applied to your history. Nobody is going to accept a headline. They want the reasoning underneath it, which is exactly what your written story bank is for.

Frequently asked questions

How many Leadership Principles are there?

The list has grown over the years, from ten in the early 2000s to fourteen and then sixteen. Because it changes, read the current list on Amazon's own careers site rather than a summary somewhere else. Evans notes that Amazon added principles as recently as the 2020s and that interviewers historically ask less about the newest additions, so weight your prep toward the long-standing ones like customer obsession, ownership, dive deep, bias for action, and have backbone.

Should I say the name of the principle in my answer?

Only if it fits the sentence. Evans says a really skilled candidate can work the words in and that if you can't do it naturally, don't worry about it. Interviewers are listening for behavior, not vocabulary. A place it does work well is the questions-for-us section at the end, where you can say a specific principle resonates with you and explain why.

What if I don't have a story for one of the principles?

Go further back in your history before giving up, including school, volunteer work, or side projects if you're early in your career. If there's genuinely nothing, don't fabricate one, because the follow-up questions will take it apart. Answer honestly with the closest real thing you have and say what you'd do in that situation now. A thin answer costs you less than an invented one that collapses under drilling.

How long should each answer be?

Aim for about two minutes on the opening answer, then let the interviewer steer. You're leaving room on purpose. They have follow-ups queued and a limited hour, and a six-minute monologue eats the time they need to gather evidence.

Is the STAR method still the right structure?

Yes, though treat it as a checklist for what to include rather than a script to recite. Carr describes using it to understand everything from the situation to the result and everything in between. Just make sure the actions and the result get most of your airtime, because that's what the interviewer is scoring.

How much prep time does this take?

Writing eight to twelve stories properly takes a few evenings. Saying them out loud with someone asking follow-ups takes several more sessions, and that's the part people skip. Evans points out the asymmetry bluntly: your interviewer may have done a thousand interviews and you've done ten, so practice is how you close some of that gap.

Does this apply to companies other than Amazon?

The principles themselves show up elsewhere, since Anderson says he knows companies that took Amazon's list directly into their own interview process. More usefully, the preparation method works anywhere: build a mapped story bank, put numbers in it, and rehearse the follow-ups. Your prep plan on one page

Three to four weeks out

  • Read the current Leadership Principles on Amazon's careers site and write one sentence per principle in your own words.
  • Ask your recruiter which rounds you're getting and roughly what each covers. They answer this happily.
  • List eight to twelve stories from the last three to five years, in a sentence each.

Two weeks out

  • Write each story out in full: situation, task, your actions, result with numbers, what you'd do differently.
  • Build the grid of stories against principles and find your gaps.
  • Hunt down the metrics you're missing: old dashboards, launch emails, former colleagues.

The final week

  • Say every story out loud with someone asking the follow-up list, especially who disagreed with you and what the numbers were.
  • Cut each opening answer down to about two minutes.
  • Check that you're saying I where you mean I, and rerecord any story where you can't hear your own decisions.
  • Prepare two or three questions to ask, including one tied to a principle you genuinely care about.

On the day

  • Vary your stories across interviewers, and keep the same numbers in the same story every time.
  • Answer, then stop, and let them drill.
  • Assume every interviewer's notes count equally, because they do.
  • Protect your energy across the full day. The last interview is scored the same as the first.
