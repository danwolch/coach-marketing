Shopify interview preparation: what they hire for and how to get ready

Shopify's engineering and product leaders have said a lot on the record about their hiring bar and their culture. Almost none of it matches standard interview advice.

14 min read

Did you find a loop structure on a forum and start rehearsing tidy stories about metrics you moved? That prep might be fine, but it can also miss the thing Shopify's own leaders spend the most time talking about, which is a bar and a working style that a lot of candidates would not enjoy.

This article covers what Shopify leaders say they hire for, how to prepare for a pairing-style process, and why writing matters there more than at most companies. It also covers where Shopify's stated approach contradicts standard career advice, and the questions to ask so you can decide whether you'd thrive. Read the honesty section first, because it tells you what this playbook does not cover.

Everything here traces to a named Shopify leader speaking on the record: Farhan Thawar, VP and head of engineering, who runs the internship program and redesigned Shopify's compensation system; Tobi Lütke, Shopify's founder and CEO; Archie Abrams, VP of product and head of growth; and Brandon Chu, who was VP of product there for seven years. Every quote below plays the exact moment it was said, or carries the timestamp if only audio exists.

What this playbook can and can't tell you

We don't have reliable current information about Shopify's interview loop mechanics, so we're not going to invent any. Round counts, question formats, take-home policies, and whether a given team pairs with you live all change from year to year and from org to org. If you read a blog post claiming to know Shopify's exact 2026 loop, treat it as one person's experience on one team.

What we do have is a lot of on-the-record material from Shopify leaders about the bar they're aiming at and the culture you'd be joining. That's what this playbook is about. Ask your recruiter for the loop structure, because they'll tell you, and use the rest of this to prepare for what the loop is measuring. For the round shapes that are common across companies, the engineering interview guide covers the formats you're most likely to get.

What Shopify leaders say they hire for

Farhan Thawar runs the hiring process at Shopify and doesn't think interviews work very well.

The dirty little secret about interviews. The player below is queued to 63:30, the exact moment Farhan Thawar says it. Watch on YouTube.

"There's a dirty little secret, right? Interviews are not a good predictor of performance. We know this. We know this from studies. Everyone at their company knows this, where somebody interviewed well, wasn't as good in the job, or the opposite. Didn't interview well and then came in and was phenomenal."
Farhan Thawar, VP and head of engineering at Shopify · Lenny's Podcast

That belief changes what the interview is for. If you assume interviews are weak predictors, you stop trying to make them a perfect filter and you start trying to make them look like the job. Thawar uses a race car analogy for this, and he's explicit that the real evaluation continues after you start.

Put them in the car. The player below is queued to 65:05, the exact moment Farhan Thawar explains the race car analogy and what happens in the first 30, 60, and 90 days. Watch on YouTube.

"If I told you, hey, I want to go hire the best race car driver, there's not really that many questions you could ask them, except for put them in the car. And so the same thing happens with us. Of course we have to do interviews, but we do really spend time in the 30, 60, 90 days to make sure that the things that they are bringing actually lines up with what we need at Shopify."
Farhan Thawar, VP and head of engineering at Shopify · Lenny's Podcast

He's blunt about what that means for you. He says a fast, chaotic environment isn't for everyone, and that if it isn't a fit, both sides benefit from finding out quickly. He also says Shopify wants to be the best 10,000-person company rather than a much bigger one. Take that at face value when you're deciding how hard to push.

One named interview step does come up. Thawar describes a stage called the life story, and what it's built to find.

The life story step, and why your resume should answer "why". The player below is queued to 67:40, the exact moment Farhan Thawar describes the stage and the line about resumes that stuck with him. Watch on YouTube.

"We have this interview step called the life story, where we try to figure out, are all the experiences you've had up until now... does it show that you are a curious person with range? ... Somebody said, you know what I don't like about resumes? It tells you what you did, but it doesn't tell you why you did those things. Your resume should be a why."
Farhan Thawar, VP and head of engineering at Shopify · Lenny's Podcast

So if you get a stage that walks through your whole history, don't recite your resume in order. Have a reason ready for every move you made. Why you left, why you picked the next thing, what you were curious about, what you went and learned on your own. Thawar is explicitly reading for curiosity and range, and he mentions that David Epstein's book Range changed his mind about specialists. A career that looks scattered is not automatically a problem here, as long as you can explain the logic behind each jump.

Tobi Lütke frames the bar in a different way, and it's worth holding next to Thawar's version because it tells you what a "good hire" is supposed to produce.

What you're actually hiring for. The player below is queued to 58:25, the exact moment Tobi Lütke describes the millions of small decisions a company makes and what that implies about hiring. Watch on YouTube.

"Shopify probably makes millions of decisions every day. Write this code this way. Yes, I'm going to add this unit test, maybe I'm skipping the unit test, might be the difference between a future production outage... you're hiring people who make excellent decisions."
Tobi Lütke, founder and CEO of Shopify · Lenny's Podcast

That's a useful lens for every answer you give. When you tell a story about past work, spend most of it on the small judgment calls you made and why, rather than on the outcome. The outcome only proves you were there, and the reasoning is what they say they're actually buying.

Thawar also says he's a big fan of job trials, and that Shopify runs them at scale through the internship program, which he said would take on a thousand interns in 2025. If you're early in your career, that's a real door, and it's a door where four months of work product replaces eight hours of interviews.

What they mean when they say intensity

Shopify leaders use the word intensity constantly, and it doesn't mean what people usually assume. Thawar's version is about density of work inside normal hours.

One hour is an hour. The player below is queued to 19:55, the exact moment Farhan Thawar explains what he means by intensity. Watch on YouTube.

"I have this fundamental belief that one hour is an hour. It's the same hour. If you spend an hour, I spend an hour, it's the same time that goes by. And if I just expend more calories in that hour... let's say we both work nine to five. If I can just get more done in the nine to five, the time has elapsed the same for us, but I just got more done. And that allows me to be like, hey, I'm going to take my kid to soccer."
Farhan Thawar, VP and head of engineering at Shopify · Lenny's Podcast

He goes further in the same conversation. He says that if you don't have two or three big projects on fire, you're probably not pushing hard enough, and that you want a little chaos at the edge. He also says comfort feels like stability but discomfort actually gives you stability, because you're constantly learning.

Two things follow for your prep. First, if an interviewer asks how you work, an answer built around long hours won't get the reaction you expect. Talk about how you compress a task and what you cut to protect the hours that matter. Second, if you're asked about a project that went badly, don't sand off the chaos. Someone who says they've only ever worked on things that went smoothly is telling this particular company that they've never taken a real risk.

How to prepare for a pairing-style interview

Thawar calls pair programming the most underutilized management tool in engineering, and he's put money behind that belief, paying for two people to sit at one computer. Shopify isn't a full-time pairing shop, and he says so plainly: it's more like a four to eight hour a week pairing culture, plus pairing during incidents and on unfamiliar parts of the codebase. But the mental model behind pairing tells you exactly what a live problem-solving round is measuring.

The limit isn't typing speed. The player below is queued to 23:00, the exact moment Farhan Thawar explains why two engineers on one computer isn't a waste. Watch on YouTube.

"It's not about lines of code. The throughput limiter is not hands on keyboard. It's not like we're both sitting there and the limiter is us trying to get through the keystrokes onto the screen. The limiter is, where is the good, elegant solution? How do we think through the problem and build the right solution for the problem at hand?"
Farhan Thawar, VP and head of engineering at Shopify · Lenny's Podcast

He also tells the story of Tobi Lütke and CTO Cody Fauser pairing with a one-hour timer, deleting all the code and keeping the tests if they didn't finish, on the theory that a solution that takes longer than an hour means the design is wrong. That's a strong signal about how they think, and about how comfortable they are throwing work away.

If your round involves working through a problem live with someone, here's how to prepare for it:

Practice the behavior, not just the answers

  • Solve problems out loud with another person, not silently on your own. The skill being tested is joint problem-solving, and it's a different skill from solving alone.
  • Narrate your reasoning as you go: what you think the problem is, what approach you're considering, what you're unsure about.
  • Ask your partner questions and actually use their answers. A pairing round where you ignore the other person is a failed pairing round.
  • Practice abandoning an approach mid-problem. Say "this is getting complicated, let me try a different shape" out loud, then do it. Sunk cost is the exact thing Thawar says is hard and valuable to overcome.
  • Get comfortable with silence while you think. Announce it: "give me thirty seconds on this."

Get comfortable looking dumb

  • Thawar says he's been told to his face, by successful people, that his was the stupidest question they'd ever heard, and that his response was to move on to the next question. He says his goal is to understand the content, not to avoid annoying anyone. That is the posture a pairing round rewards.
  • Ask the clarifying question you're embarrassed to ask. Candidates who guess at requirements to look confident tend to solve the wrong problem.
  • Say "I don't know" and then say what you'd do to find out.

If rambling under pressure is your failure mode, the playbook on rambling and the one on organizing your thoughts before you speak both drill the same muscle a pairing round tests.

Why writing matters more here than at most companies

Brandon Chu spent seven years at Shopify, ending as a VP of product, and his blog posts on product management are widely read. He says writing did more for his career than anything else, and his reason is about thinking rather than publishing.

Lenny's Podcast: Brandon Chu on building product at Shopify. This one is audio only. The quotes are at 27:26 and 31:29; the player starts from the top, or open it on Apple Podcasts cued to 27:26.

"I figured out everything in those posts at the exact moment I wrote them. It was the writing process itself that actually allowed me to solidify those mental models and the frameworks in my mind."
Brandon Chu, then VP of product at Shopify · Lenny's Podcast (27:26, audio)

He puts the obligation on anyone doing the job, whether or not anything gets published: "You owe your team, your peers, your stakeholders that level of clarity. So even if you're writing and throw it away, you've created the clarity in your mind and you can articulate it as such" (31:29, audio). He also describes the practical effect at Shopify, where the company was hiring so fast that people who joined his team already knew how he thought because they'd read his posts.

For your prep, that means two things. Anything you send them in writing is part of the evaluation, so treat the application questions and the follow-up email as work samples rather than formalities. And if you've written anything public about your craft, put it where they'll find it, because Chu describes writing as the thing that built his trust with Tobi Lütke and pulled other people into the company.

Where Shopify contradicts the standard advice

This is the part worth sitting with, because Shopify's leaders say things that cut against advice we and most other people give. Naming the tension is more useful than pretending it isn't there.

Metrics and goals

Most interview prep tells you to quantify everything and to lead with the metric you moved. Archie Abrams, who runs growth, says the core of the company doesn't work that way.

KPIs and OKRs are banned. The player below is queued to 48:25, the exact moment Archie Abrams explains what most Shopify teams are held to instead. Watch on YouTube.

"It's funny, we rant against KPIs. They're basically banned, and OKRs are banned, and all that. Certainly in growth you have the metrics, but they take a different form. And then in core, it truly is: do we have conviction that this is the right technical foundation to build the future of commerce?"
Archie Abrams, VP of product and head of growth at Shopify · Lenny's Podcast

He's honest about the cost too, saying conversations get extremely subjective about what the right thing to do is, and that a small group including Tobi Lütke holds the taste bar. So keep your numbers, because a Shopify growth interviewer will still want them. But have a second version of every story that runs on conviction: what you believed and why, and what you'd have done if the dashboard had told you nothing useful. If your entire professional identity is "I hit the number," this is a place where that answer does less work than usual.

One-on-ones

Our one-on-one playbook tells you to protect the recurring meeting with your manager and to run it deliberately, and we stand by that at most companies. Thawar ran the opposite experiment, at a company before Shopify.

No scheduled one-on-ones, and 120 direct reports. The player below is queued to 77:20, the exact moment Farhan Thawar explains the setup he ran at his previous company. Watch on YouTube.

"I did two things famously. One, I had a lot of direct reports, and two, I did not do any scheduled one-on-ones, because you can't have 120 direct reports and do scheduled one-on-ones, because you're never doing anything but one-on-ones. So I said, I'm going to be around a lot because I don't have a lot of one-on-ones. So we can do unscheduled one-on-ones."
Farhan Thawar, VP and head of engineering at Shopify · Lenny's Podcast

Two honest caveats. This was his previous company, in roughly 2009 to 2013, and he says his biggest investor pushed back by asking whether it would work at 400 people, at which point he added structure and made a few directors with 40 reports each. He also says Shopify has guardrails and he doesn't carry 120 reports there. So this isn't a description of Shopify's current management model.

The part that transfers is the underlying claim: he looked back at 151 weekly one-on-ones over three years at Microsoft and concluded the useful conversations were the unscheduled ones, when he knocked on his manager's door with an actual problem. If you're interviewing at Shopify, or anywhere that talks like this, prepare for a manager relationship where the burden of raising things sits with you. Ask in the interview how your prospective manager runs it. And if the honest answer is that you do your best work with a protected weekly slot, that's real information about fit, and you shouldn't hide it.

Meetings and process

Shopify deletes every recurring internal meeting with more than two people once a year at a random time, then bans new recurring meetings for two weeks. Thawar describes it in the same conversation and says the point is to break the inertia behind meetings that exist only because they've always existed. If you're used to influence flowing through a standing meeting series, expect to build it differently there.

Questions that tell you whether you'd actually thrive there

Thawar's own advice for job seekers is to write down a framework of what you care about, before a recruiter's pitch has a chance to confuse you. He says title, company, and money all distract, that he sent his framework to a recruiter once to explain why a role didn't fit, and that when he notices his framework is being violated he resigns, sometimes without another job lined up (15:53 and 17:07 in the same episode). You don't have to be that extreme to use the idea. Write your version down before your first call, then ask questions that test it.

Ask about intensity honestly

  • "How many things are on fire on this team right now, and is that normal here?"
  • "What did the last three months look like for someone in this role, hour by hour on a typical day?"
  • "When was the last time this team threw away several months of work because the world changed, and how did people take it?"

Ask about how you'd be managed

  • "Do we have a recurring one-on-one, and if not, how do people here get unblocked?"
  • "How would I know if I'm doing well in the first 90 days? Who tells me, and how often?"
  • "How does this team decide what to build when there's no metric to point at?"

Ask about the work itself

  • "How much of the week is spent pairing or working side by side with someone?"
  • "What gets written down here, and who reads it?"
  • "Who holds the quality bar on this team, and how do they communicate it?"

Listen for whether the answers sound proud or resigned. A team that talks about the chaos with energy is a very different place to work from one that talks about putting up with it. And notice your own reaction, because Thawar's whole point is that the environment is genuinely not for everyone, and that finding out fast is good for both sides.

What to do before you apply

The most actionable thing Thawar says has nothing to do with interviewing. It's about what job seekers do with their time.

Build on the platform instead of sending more resumes. The player below is queued to 6:40, the exact moment Farhan Thawar gives his standard advice to job seekers. Watch on YouTube.

"I meet lots of job seekers and I go, what are you doing to try and find a job? I'm sending out 10 resumes a day. I'm like, okay, that sounds kind of easy. Are you really learning anything from sending out 10 resumes a day? ... Why don't you look at the API and build something? Build a Shopify app, build an admin extension. Even if you don't get a job at Shopify, you've learned something. You've built something, you have things in your GitHub repo now."
Farhan Thawar, VP and head of engineering at Shopify · Lenny's Podcast

This is the head of engineering telling you what would impress him. Ship something on Shopify's platform, even something small, and now you have a real answer when someone asks why this company. You'll also have opinions about the developer experience, which is a much better conversation than reciting the company's mission statement back at them.

Frequently asked questions

Does Shopify still do pair programming interviews?

We don't know, and we're not going to guess. Thawar describes Shopify as roughly a four to eight hour a week pairing culture rather than a full-time pairing shop, and he describes pairing on incidents and unfamiliar code. Whether that shows up in your loop depends on the team and the year. Ask your recruiter what each round involves, and prepare for live collaborative problem-solving either way, because it's the format most likely to catch you off guard.

What is the life story interview?

Thawar names it as a step where they walk through your experiences looking for evidence that you're a curious person with range. His framing is that a resume tells you what someone did but not why, and the why is the interesting part. Prepare a reason for every transition in your history.

Is Shopify a hard place to work?

Their leaders say yes, in public, on purpose. Thawar says you should have two or three big projects on fire, and calls the environment chaotic and fast-moving. Abrams describes a place where teams throw away months of work when the world changes. Nobody is hiding this. Decide whether it appeals to you before you spend weeks preparing.

Should I quantify my achievements if KPIs are banned there?

Yes, keep the numbers. Abrams says growth teams do use metrics, they just take a different form, and no interviewer penalizes a clear result. Add a layer on top: what you believed and why, and what call you'd have made without the data. That's the reasoning Lütke describes hiring for.

How do I get a Shopify internship?

Thawar treats the internship program as Shopify's real job trial at scale, and said the 2025 program would take roughly a thousand interns. He explicitly contrasts four months of work product against eight hours of interviews. If you're early in your career, that path is worth more of your attention than a perfectly tuned resume.

What if I've never worked somewhere this intense?

Say so, and be specific about what you have done. Thawar's own advice is to take the harder path, and he points out that even when the hard thing doesn't work out you've learned something and worked with sharp people. An honest answer about a stretch you took, including what went badly, fits their stated values better than a polished record with no risk in it.

Your Shopify prep checklist

Before you apply

  • Build something on the Shopify platform, even something small, and put it somewhere they can see it.
  • Write down your own framework of what you care about in a job, before any recruiter call.
  • Treat every written thing you send as a work sample.

While you prepare

  • Ask your recruiter exactly what each round involves. Don't rely on secondhand accounts of the loop.
  • Have a why for every move in your career history, ready to say out loud.
  • Rebuild your best stories around the judgment calls rather than the outcomes.
  • Prepare a version of each story that runs on conviction instead of metrics.
  • Practice solving problems out loud with another person, at least three times, timed.

During the loop

  • Narrate your reasoning, including the parts you're unsure about.
  • Ask the clarifying question you're embarrassed to ask.
  • Abandon a bad approach out loud rather than defending it.
  • Talk about how you compress work, not how many hours you put in.
  • Don't sand the chaos out of your failure stories.

Before you accept

  • Ask how people get unblocked, and whether recurring one-on-ones exist on that team.
  • Ask what the first 90 days are evaluated on, and who tells you how it's going.
  • Notice whether the answers sound energized or worn down, and take your own reaction seriously.
